1\. Purpose and selection logic

CI wants at least three brand-delivery support models for emerging protected areas, ranging from a grant-funded technical assistance retainer that parks draw down on to a commercially run brand platform inspired by Leading Hotels of the World. Activity One asks for a review of African regional and multi-destination initiatives, naming Africa’s Eden and Boundless Southern Africa, alongside global multi-brand platforms. This pass builds the long-list that review will draw on. It is not yet the POV.

Candidates had to pass five tests; first, aggregation, meaning one brand, service or standard serving many sites or owners. Second, relevance to protected areas, nature or heritage. Third, a legible funding model that shows who pays and why. Fourth, governance that spans jurisdictions or stakeholder groups. Fifth, a record long enough to show durability or an instructive failure. Thirty-one benchmarks passed, nine of them added in this draft. We grouped them by what the central entity does, since that choice drives cost, control and who pays.

Scale shapes the answer. The Africa Keystone Protected Area Partnership launched in September 2025 around 162 protected areas, and its partners put the cost of managing them well at US\$1.2bn a year. CI’s tourism team has narrowed its focus to 15 emerging areas. A model built for 15 must be able to stretch towards 162.

2\. Seven archetypes along CI’s solution spectrum

CI’s brief describes a spectrum from a service parks use to a platform that sells on their behalf. The benchmarks fall into seven archetypes along it. The first five describe what a CI facility could do at its centre; the last two supply brand architecture lessons and revenue modules that any model can carry.

2.1 What changed in draft 2

Three refinements follow from the new candidates. First, standards now split in two. Site standards certify places, and Blue Flag joins the Geoparks, the ECST and Slovenia Green; enterprise marks certify businesses and products, a new archetype holding Fair Trade Tourism and Certified Wildlife Friendly. Second, place marketers and curators now carry sub-types, because the new cases showed different economics inside each. A member-funded club of places such as Alpine Pearls works nothing like a state-funded national brand such as Visit Rwanda. A collection hosted on an existing platform, such as Beyond Green or SLH’s Considerate Collection, avoids the distribution costs an independent consortium must carry. Third, three design choices cut across every archetype and belong in the option design.

Began as a French pilot in 1985.</td>

<td>FEE, based in Copenhagen, owns the standard; national operators and juries implement it. Global partnership agreements with UNEP and UN Tourism.</td>
<td>The strongest proof that one standard can scale across jurisdictions through national operators. Annual renewal keeps the flag honest. In Africa, Morocco runs it through the Mohammed VI Foundation for the Protection of the Environment.</td>

6\. Early signals for the POV

These are hypotheses to test, not conclusions. Each rests on evidence already in the long-list.

***Aggregation saves most in the back office.***

The clearest cost-efficiency evidence in the set is operational, not promotional. Harpers Ferry Center gives 400+ park units one design system and prints 24 to 28 million brochures a year at a few cents each. Namibia’s 86 conservancies all use the same Event Book monitoring system and report through one annual national review. NRT pools governance, security and enterprise support for 43 conservancies. None of them asks sites to surrender their own identity. That points to a shared brand kit, studio and data service as the first layer of any CI model.

***In Africa, the aggregation layer often already exists.***

NACSO links the organisations serving 86 Namibian conservancies. MMWCA speaks for 24 Mara conservancies, some 16,500 landowners and 50 tourism partners. NRT serves 43 conservancies. CI already knows this terrain: in 2020 it set up a US\$5m bridge-loan fund with MMWCA to keep lease payments flowing when tourism collapsed. CI’s terms ask the model to fill gaps rather than duplicate efforts. In several target countries that means working through these associations, not around them.

***Africa’s best-known lodges are already curated; the gap is the emerging areas.***

A third of Beyond Green’s 24 founding members were African safari camps, most of them from &Beyond and Wilderness. Five of National Geographic Unique Lodges’ 24 charter members were African, and Relais & Châteaux counts lodges in Zambia and Botswana. The commercial collections have already found Africa’s marquee products. The brand gap sits with the emerging areas, which argues for bringing their lodges into existing collections, or hosting a ‘Keystone collection’ on an existing platform, before founding a new consortium.

***Brands last when someone pays for what they deliver.***

LHW has operated since 1928 and Relais & Châteaux since 1954 because independent members buy distribution and demand; Relais & Châteaux members reported €3.308bn of business in 2025 and applications to join rose 14%. The publicly or donor-seeded networks in the set have mostly contracted or been restructured. The ECST fell from 164 areas to 91. Alpine Pearls fell from about 24 villages to 18. The Commission redesigned EDEN after a 2019 evaluation. Fair Trade Tourism, which scaled across eight countries on Swiss government funding, now works through a partnership with SATSA. For a commercial platform, CI’s paying customer is more likely the lodge or operator than the park authority.

***Credibility comes from standards with consequences.***

Geoparks lose their status on a red card after four-yearly revalidation. Blue Flag is re-awarded every year and monitored through the season by national operators. LHW admits members only by nomination and inspection. The Long Run gives Fellow Members about five years to reach its GER standard. For 15 areas of uneven capacity, a tiered pathway of the kind Slovenia Green runs from bronze to platinum can reconcile inclusion with credibility.

***National brands must be partners, not rivals.***

Tourism Australia sells its collectives under ‘There’s Nothing Like Australia’. The Slovenian Tourist Board owns Slovenia Green outright. KAZA offers operators a brand hub so they co-brand rather than compete. Rwanda spent more than £80m on its Arsenal deal alone; a park brand there would sit beneath Visit Rwanda, not beside it. CI’s model should give national tourism boards product and proof for their own campaigns, not a parallel identity.

***Consumer memberships are real money, but cyclical and slow to build.***

National Trust memberships earned £309.4m in 2024/25, yet slipped 0.4% as cost-pressed visitors paid on the day. LHW abandoned its paid Leaders Club tier in 2021. The Wild Card works because five agencies pooled 80+ parks around a large domestic market. For CI, tiered memberships and wild cards look like second-phase revenue, once enough parks and a regional customer base exist.

***Outside money comes with strings.***

Brand USA’s 1:1 match unlocked up to US\$100m a year in public funds against industry cash and in-kind contributions. It shrank when COVID collapsed ESTA receipts, and again when Congress cut the match cap to US\$20m in 2025. The Keystone Partnership already uses match logic: the Rob Walton Foundation will match GEF-9 resources directed to Keystone areas with up to US\$50m, with CI providing technical assistance. Sponsorship can also run the other way. Rwanda pays its football clubs for reach, and in February 2026 the DRC urged those clubs to drop the deals. For CI’s emerging areas, sponsorship is more likely a cost of visibility than a revenue line until the brand has an audience worth selling.

7\. What this means for the Activity One method

Four choices follow; first, every deep-dive should follow the nine headings CI uses for model design, from the offer and expected results through cash flows, governance and funders to stakeholder pros and cons, so benchmark evidence drops straight into the options. Second, the matrix in Section 4 becomes the scoring frame for the three or more models, anchored on CI’s own design aims. Third, the archetypes and the three cross-cutting design choices give the option set a natural shape: a shared-service model delivered with existing conservancy associations, a standard-and-network model and a commercial model that hosts a Keystone collection on an existing platform before building one, with revenue engines as modules any option can carry. Fourth, interviews should prioritise the secretariats behind Boundless Southern Africa, Africa’s Eden Tourism, KAZA, NRT, NACSO, MMWCA and The Long Run, plus Preferred Hotel Group and SLH on hosting terms, since none of them publishes cost or outcome data.

The budget sets the depth. At US\$40,000 over 12 weeks, fully desk-based, eight case studies (two of them clustered) and five light-touch checks is the most the programme can carry.

8\. Gaps and next research steps

Four evidence gaps matter most. First, commercial terms: LHW, Relais & Châteaux, SLH, Preferred Hotel Group, National Geographic and The Long Run do not publish member fees, yet the economics of CI’s ‘complex version’ depend on them. Second, outcomes: we found activity reports for Boundless Southern Africa and KAZA but no published measures of recognition or bookings, and Visit Rwanda’s claimed returns rest on government figures without independent attribution. Third, association budgets: what NRT, NACSO and MMWCA spend per conservancy and where the money comes from. Fourth, CI’s list of 15 priority areas, which determines the national tourism boards, co-management partners and source markets the models must serve; we should request it at inception.

Two candidates surfaced for a later pass. Ecotourism Kenya’s eco-rating and Botswana’s ecotourism certification came up through Fair Trade Tourism’s mutual recognition agreements; they are national standards CI could recognise rather than replace. The Kenya Wildlife Conservancies Association, of which MMWCA is one of 12 regional associations, is the obvious national-level check.
