### West Coast (p58–p65)

1. West Coast accommodation concentrates on the Langebaan–Saldanha–St Helena Bay coastline and is dominated by self-catering and guesthouse stock with limited large-scale hotel capacity; inland municipalities have limited graded accommodation and premium eco-lodges and hotels are scarce outside coastal towns. Map legend: Guesthouses & B&Bs, Hotels & lodges, Self-catering units, Camping & caravans, Backpacker hostels; no counts printed (p58). FA10, FA15, FA01.

2. West Coast dining and retail centre on the Langebaan–Vredenburg–Saldanha service triangle; Paternoster, Darling and Clanwilliam add small-scale culinary appeal; inland dining and retail are limited and "local food tourism remains underdeveloped" (p59). FA10, FA11.

3. "The R27 and N7 corridors carry virtually all visitor access" to the West Coast; northern West Coast municipalities have limited public transport and no passenger rail; the Saldanha–Cape Town rail corridor is freight-focused; the slide proposes shuttle services, EV charging infrastructure and improved visitor information (p60). FA09, FA19.

4. The West Coast / Cederberg route scores EMERGING overall on the international readiness assessment: Air & road access Developing ("Easy roads, long distances, no airport"); Accommodation range Gap ("Thin outside Langebaan and Paternoster"); Safety & support Ready ("Low-crime, relaxed fishing towns"); Digital & bookability Gap ("Weak digital distribution and payments") (p62). Route card: 120 km approx from Cape Town, 280 km approx route length, 3.5 h end-to-end drive, suggested stay 2–4 days, drive order Langebaan · Saldanha · Velddrif · Lambert's Bay (p72). FA03, FA10, FA07.

5. The West Coast asset base is rated 2.5/5 overall, with pillar scores Attractiveness 3.2, Readiness 2.7, Accessibility 1.7, and category scores Natural 3.2, Events 2.9, Cultural & heritage 2.9, Accommodation 2.6, Shopping 2.5, Eatery 2.5, Visitor amenities 2.4, Commercial & leisure 2.3, Enabling structure 2.2, Transport 2.2, Activity 2.2 (p63, bar values printed on chart). The slide's lead statement: "Known for seasonal nature-tourism identity, The West Coast also experiences significant operator under-investment" (p63). FA10, FA09, FA15.

6. The West Coast has the "Lowest operator investment ratio in the province", no year-round demand anchor, extreme seasonality concentrated in the wildflower season (Aug–Sep), off-season operator closures, limited healthcare and digital reliability in smaller coastal towns, and "Climate change threatens wildflower season predictability" (p64). FA02, FA14, FA15, FA19.

7. West Coast planned investment comprises a R4bn Transnet Saldanha port upgrade with provisional cruise-berth trials (3 liner calls), the R693m Malmesbury Bypass (completed) linking Saldanha/IDZ to the N7, N1 and N2, harbour and waterfront redevelopment in Lambert's Bay, Elandsbaai and Laaiplek/Pelican, and SA Connect rural broadband rollout; remaining gaps are no committed investment in accommodation capacity or payments infrastructure, no airport planned, a provisional (not committed) cruise berth, and multi-day itineraries dependent on private operators (p65). No dates are printed on p65 for these items. FA08, FA15, FA19, FA03.

### Overberg (p66–p76)

8. The Overberg records ~675,000 annual visitors to participating attractions, a 58% overnight share, 86% domestic and 14% international visitors, and contributes 3% to GDP (p66). The period is not stated. FA10, FA02.

9. The Overberg asset base is 6,176 total assets, 12.3% of Western Cape total tourism infrastructure, split Accommodation 3,214 (11.2%), Attractions 1,183 (9.6%), Eateries 1,209 (9.4%), Events 254 (13.8%), Amenities 272 (13.4%) (p67; percentages read from gauge labels). The five components sum to 6,132, not 6,176 (difference 44). The slide subtitle says "over 6,176 assets". FA10, FA15.

10. Whale-watching is the Overberg's "globally recognised anchor product" and the whale-watching season "drives extreme demand peaks in September-Nov"; access is "highly car-dependent with no public tourism transport connecting Hermanus, Stanford, Elim, or Agulhas"; event-driven tourism "temporarily solves accessibility constraints by motivating visitors to plan transport specifically" (p67). FA02, FA09, FA12.

11. The Hermanus–Stanford–Gansbaai marine corridor anchors the Overberg attraction base; the Rûens interior has relatively low attraction density; accommodation clusters in Hermanus and along the whale coast with "limited conferencing-scale capacity" (p68, p69). FA02, FA10.

12. The N2 and R43 carry Overberg tourism access and keep it self-drive dependent; the Cape Town–Caledon rail corridor is freight only; "There are no operational passenger rail services serving tourism destinations in region"; public transport is limited beyond the N2 corridor (p71). FA09.

13. The Whale Coast / Overberg route scores DEVELOPING overall, with all four criteria Developing: Air & road access ("Good roads from Cape Town, no airport"), Accommodation range ("Strong at Hermanus, thin further east"), Safety & support ("Generally safe, support concentrated west"), Digital & bookability ("Hermanus bookable, the rest uneven") (p73). Route card: 120 km approx from Cape Town, 230 km approx route length, 3.5 h end-to-end drive, suggested stay 3–5 days, drive order Gordon's Bay · Hermanus · Stanford · Bredasdorp · Swellendam (p61). FA03, FA10, FA12.

14. The Overberg asset base is rated 2.7/5 overall with category scores Natural 3.3, Accommodation 3.1, Events 3.1, Cultural & heritage 2.9, Activity 2.9, Eatery 2.8, Commercial & leisure 2.8, Visitor amenities 2.5, Shopping 2.5, Transport 2.4, Enabling structure 2.3; road access is via the N2, R43 and R44 with "no integrated tourism mobility network" (p74). No pillar (Attractiveness/Readiness/Accessibility) numbers are printed on p74. FA10, FA09.

15. Overberg gap areas include whale-season peaks creating "operator over-reliance on a single window", "Climate change risks to marine and coastal product (whale migration patterns, sea levels)", peak-season municipal services strain on Hermanus roads and parking, "Weak accessibility across all categories creating a uniform structural deficit" and an inland investment shortfall widening the coastal-inland gap (p75). No dates printed. FA15, FA03, FA02.

### Central Karoo and Route 62 (p77–p87)

18. The Central Karoo is "The province's smallest tourism base at 1,518 assets", 3.0% of Western Cape total tourism infrastructure, split Accommodation 622 (3.0%), Attractions 281 (2.3%), Eateries 325 (2.5%), Events 124 (6.8%), Amenities 124 (printed "%7.0", read as 7.0%) (p78). The five components sum to 1,476, not 1,518 (difference 42). "Commercial and leisure attractions are near-absent lowest in the province"; occupancy is "heavily tied to transit traffic" (p78). FA10, FA15.

19. The N1 is the Central Karoo's only reliable transport corridor; there is no public transport, limited healthcare and weak digital infrastructure (p78, p82); the Cape Town–Johannesburg rail line passes through Beaufort West "supporting luxury rail tourism via Blue Train and Rovos Rail"; "Most tourism destinations are inaccessible by passenger rail" (p82). The transport map legend carries a single class, "Fuel & roadside services" (p82). FA09, FA19.

Route 62 / Klein Karoo scores DEVELOPING overall: Air & road access Developing ("No nearby airport, gravel mountain passes"); Accommodation range Developing ("Charming but thin at international grade"); Safety & support Developing ("Safe but sparse emergency support"); Digital & bookability Gap ("Low online bookability inland") (p84). Route card: 160 km approx from Cape Town, 330 km approx route length, 4.5 h end-to-end drive, suggested stay 3–5 days, drive order Robertson · Montagu · Barrydale · Ladismith · Calitzdorp · Oudtshoorn; stops A4.1 Robertson, A4.2 Montagu, A4.3 Tradouw, A4.4 Seweweeks., A4.5 Calitzdorp, A4.6 Swartberg, A4.7 Oudtshoorn, A4.8 Meiringspt (Tradouw, Swartberg and Meiringspoort are drawn in orange with dashed connectors; the key for this styling is not printed) (p83). The slide subtitle reads "World-class highly distinctive tourism assets along the N2" (the district's corridor is the N1 elsewhere in the deck). No pillar numbers printed. FA10, FA09.

Central Karoo gap areas: "Region functions as transit corridor, not yet a tourism economy"; "Extreme summer temperatures shrink viable visitor windows"; "Critically small operator base; micro-enterprises lack scalable capacity"; "Tourism offices under-resourced; destination marketing near zero"; load-shedding and telecoms reliability constrain operators; "N1 truck congestion threatens transit market subsidising operators" (p86). ### Comparative regional analysis (p88–p90)

24. On attractions, "Cape Metro holds the volume; Garden Route and Overberg hold the counter-seasonal potential, although every region outside the Metro is being outpaced by its own assets"; Cape Metro scores 4.7 accessibility in every attraction category and the Central Karoo 1.0 in every category; Cape Metro Activity and Natural attractiveness are 4.8; Central Karoo Commercial & Leisure is 1.3 attractiveness, 1.3 readiness (p88; full table in Key tables). FA10, FA12, FA02.

25. Cape Metro "has five of province's six highest-volume attractions" and suffers "peak-season congestion at flagship sites, overflow reserves remain under-marketed"; Table Mountain, Robben Island and Kirstenbosch are "the only globally-branded landmark cluster"; Cape Winelands is anchored by 200+ wine estates; the Garden Route's 300km of coastline "fragments destination into disconnected nodes"; Hermanus is "world-class land-based whale-watching" (p88). FA12, FA05, FA10.

26. On services, "accessibility scores expose a two-tier system where strong product exists in places visitors cannot easily reach or afford"; Cape Metro Eatery and Shopping readiness are 5.0; West Coast "outperforms Cape Metro on value"; the Overberg is the "only market where demand and pricing peak in winter"; the Central Karoo is "a genuine service desert outside Prince Albert and Beaufort West" (p89). FA02, FA05, FA01.

27. On transport and enabling structures, "The further from Cape Town, the faster the infrastructure falls away and with it, the ability to convert any of the province's natural and cultural assets into a reliable visitor experience"; Cape Metro accessibility is 5.0 and Central Karoo 1.0 on all three categories; Cape Metro is the "only region with an international airport & rapid transit" but "rail functionally unusable for tourism"; the Garden Route has "no inter-town shuttle" and its airport is "the only secondary airport with scheduled passenger service"; the Overberg's "most-requested route stays unserved"; in the Central Karoo "every accessibility measure sits at the floor, no airport, rail or e-hailing" (p90). FA09, FA19, FA07.

28. Accessibility scores decay with distance from the Metro: Cape Metro 4.7 (attractions and services) and 5.0 (transport), Cape Winelands 3.3, Garden Route 3.0, Overberg 2.0, West Coast 1.7, Central Karoo 1.0. The value is identical across every category within each district with one exception: West Coast Natural accessibility is 2.0 on p88 while every other West Coast category is 1.7 (p88, p89, p90). ### Province-wide asset mapping (p91–p99)

29. Attractions concentrate along three corridors; the Cape corridor holds the most diversified base "within a compact and walkable geography"; N2 nature-corridor products "operate without interpretive content, cultural context or heritage storytelling"; Overberg and West Coast marine and wildflower assets "remain largely inactivated with no guided tours, no visitor interpretation centres and no bookable product around them"; the Central Karoo and northern inland areas are "attraction deserts with no anchor products, no established touring routes"; "MICE, Heritage sites, cultural assets and community-based tourism potential remain disconnected from any distribution or marketing channel" (p92, p93). FA03, FA11, FA13, FA02.

30. Accommodation supply concentrates in three corridors; Cape corridor stock "skews heavily to 4 and 5-star graded properties"; the Winelands is a premium cluster "with no hostel, backpacker or budget tier"; the Garden Route is "the largest non-metro accommodation corridor" but "limited on quality-graded hotels that anchor midweek, conference or business travel"; "Province-wide, there is no midscale branded product between luxury estates and basic self-catering"; "Most rural properties have no OTA presence and are invisible to the market"; West Coast, Karoo and Overberg stock has "no connecting chain to support multi-night touring between corridors and near-zero visibility on booking platforms" (p94, p95). FA01, FA02, FA03, FA15.

31. Tourism food and retail spend diffuses weakly: "Township food economies, local markets and cultural retail experiences are largely disconnected from mainstream tourism itineraries"; inland areas offer "minimal eatery presence beyond franchise fast-food"; Hermanus, Langebaan, Paternoster, Swellendam and Prince Albert are the regional dining nodes (p96, p97). FA11, FA13.

32. The province's transport system is corridor-dominated: "The N2 is the only continuous inter-regional tourism corridor, creating a single point of failure"; "Large parts of the province outside Cape Metro and the Garden Route remain effectively car-only destinations"; "minimal shuttle, shared mobility and cycling connections"; "Metrorail's tourism potential is constrained by safety perceptions and limited visitor-oriented wayfinding"; fuel stations trace the N1, N2 and R62 while airports, rail and EV charging cluster around Cape Town and the Garden Route; George Airport is the secondary aviation gateway (p98, p99). FA09, FA19.

### Rail (p100)

33. Metrorail's Western Cape network is 460km, 5 lines, 104 of 121 stations operational, with 22.7m passenger trips in FY2025; Central Line recovery is ~70% complete with Philippi–Kapteinsklip "targeted fully operational by Jan 2026 aiming for 6 trains/hour"; the Cape Town–Simon's Town line is fully operational and positioned as a tourism route with hop-on/hop-off day passes; the Southern Line strings together Muizenberg, Kalk Bay, Boulders Beach and Simon's Town; Ceres Rail steam excursions relaunched in 2024 and Rovos Rail runs Winelands and Cape routes (p100). FA09, FA12.

34. Rail investment plans are R21.1bn PRASA (fleet renewal, "halfway to a 600-train target by 2035", infrastructure recovery, tap-and-go ticketing), ~R1.3bn spent on the Central Line Relocation Programme, a R1.9tn national infrastructure plan over 25 years with Cape Town Metrorail the 2nd priority, and the City of Cape Town devolution bid (R123bn/30-year feasibility case); gaps the plans do not cover: no dedicated tourism rail product or marketing (Southern Line visitor branding dates to 2007), no direct rail link to Cape Town International Airport, no public rail investment beyond the Cape Metro, visitor safety and station security not an explicit line item (p100). FA09, FA15, FA19.

### Air access (p101–p109)

35. "The airport, not a shortage of visitors is what now caps Cape Town's tourism growth"; of Cape Town International's seven parts, 2 are full/blocking growth (the runway/airfield and the domestic arrivals hall), 4 are under strain (aircraft parking, the international terminal, domestic departures, roads/parking) and 1 is a clear strength (airline network: 24 airlines and 35 routes); the fix depends on ACSA's ~R11.3bn upgrade (p101, p102). FA07, FA19.

36. Cape Town International's single runway with no parallel taxiway caps movements at ~30 flights/hour; the international terminal is a modern 2013 building (~3m passengers/yr) congested at peak; the domestic arrivals hall was "Never rebuilt in the 2010 upgrade, chronically undersized"; "No room to grow outward. There's no space for a second runway or a bigger terminal, the fix has to move the runway itself"; the pressure concentrates in the morning/evening banks and the Nov–March season (p101, p102). FA07.

37. Cape Town International's fixes and dates: Move & rebuild the runway R6.39bn, adds ~40% more flights/hour, runway builder appointed Dec 2026, construction from ~2027, done ~2033; New domestic arrivals + departures R2.7bn, done ~2034; International terminal upgrade R853m, done ~2032; these are "the biggest part of ACSA's ~R21.7bn national spend"; terminals take 5–7 years to build; relief arrives from ~2032 against the 2035 target; recommendation to track runway milestones quarterly and commission a capacity-vs-demand check (p103). The airport handles ~11m passengers today (p109). FA07, FA16, FA19.

38. George Airport handled 911,000 two-way passengers in 2025 (+11%), already above the ~900k design limit, with ~96,000 passengers in December 2025 alone; 3 airlines (FlySafair, Airlink, CemAir) fly to Joburg, Cape Town and Durban; it is domestic-only; the terminal is "solar-powered (Africa's first)"; a lapse in renewing landing-system approvals cancelled 10+ flights in a day (Jan 2025), and "Weather & landing reliability" is rated red (full/blocking), the same rating as the passenger terminal, so George carries two red parts, not one; the runway is due for resurfacing from Oct 2026 (p104). FA07, FA09.

39. "A R310m Terminal Expansion Doubles Capacity By 2028" at George: terminal expansion R310m, capacity 900k → ~2m, ~2028; runway resurfacing ~2028 (no extra capacity); landing-system and weather upgrade ~2027; perimeter and emergency roads ~2026 (already under way); "capacity relief lands by 2028, seven years ahead of the target"; recommendation to press ACSA and ATNS on landing-system reliability and track seat growth (p105). FA07, FA15.

40. The Cape Winelands airport is a private ~R8bn phase-1 development at the old Fisantekraal airfield ~30km north of the city, with a terminal designed for 5.2m passengers/yr, a 3.5 km runway, first passengers targeted 2028 and construction from late 2026; environmental approval granted and all 5 appeals dismissed (2026); Growthpoint is equity partner but full ~R8bn funding "is not yet locked in"; airline demand is Unproven ("No firm named carrier"); R302 / R312 / R304 need upgrading for 2.5m+ passengers; airspace coordination with Cape Town International (~30km apart) to resolve; the timeline shows "Passengers ramp to ~2.5m/yr" from ~2028 (p106, p107). FA07, FA04, FA15.

41. Neither secondary airfield changes the 2035 picture: Plettenberg Bay has one short 1,220m unlit runway (daylight only), one airline on a seasonal Plett–Joburg route, the Cape Town route paused, runway and terminal under repair after a 2024 licence scare, and ~1,600m+ is needed for larger regional aircraft; Beaufort West has a paved, lit 1,483m runway, control tower and jet fuel but no scheduled flights; recommendation: do nothing further on Beaufort West, light watching brief on Plettenberg Bay (p108). FA07.

42. "Cape Town International Is The Binding Dependency": Cape Town International (Critical) handles ~11m passengers with runway relief only in the early-2030s; George (Solved) doubles to ~2m by 2028 with no capital ask; Cape Winelands (Upside bet) could add capacity sooner but has no firm airline; "The certain fix is the latest, and the earliest big-capacity option is the least certain" (p109). FA07, FA16, FA19.

### Cruise and ports (p110–p112)

43. Cape Town's single cruise berth runs at ~40% of seasonal capacity: season capacity ~150+ calls, actual 2024/25 83 calls, 127,000 passengers handled (2024/25), R1.79bn economic impact (+32% YoY), 300m max ship length at the berth; the V&A terminal is purpose-built on a 20-year concession (~R179m); constraints are the 300m ship-length ceiling and the South-Easter wind, not berth space; no shore power; recent growth "leans on Red Sea reroutes" (p110). FA08.

44. Mossel Bay (~400 km E, Garden Route day-stop, "ready now") had 21 calls and ~13,000 pax with ~23 expected next season, no cruise quay (ships anchor and tender), a shallow ~8m entrance (small-mid ships only) and a new R22m passenger facility opened Nov 2025; Saldanha Bay (~110 km NW, "a watch") had 3 trial calls, shares the ore terminal with no cruise berth, has no passenger facility ("passengers exit into an ore terminal") and strong but cargo-geared services (p111). FA08.

45. "The port investment isn't going to cruise": Saldanha's R4bn iron-ore expansion (Sept 2026) is "100% iron ore" with zero cruise; Cape Town's big project is container expansion (Dec 2026) with wind mitigation in the 2026–31 plan; only R22m (Mossel Bay Cruise Reception Facility, done Nov 2025) is cruise; priorities Cape Town Protect, Mossel Bay Back now, Saldanha Bay Watch; "cruise already delivers ~R1.79bn a year to the Western Cape, grow it through product and marketing, not capital works"; key risk is Red Sea rerouting reversing (p112). FA08, FA15, FA16.

### Key messages (p114)

46. The assessment's five key messages: (1) "The Western Cape has the tourism assets to support growth, but readiness is uneven"; (2) "Cape Town remains the engine of the system, but growth cannot keep concentrating there"; (3) "The strongest regional opportunity lies in deepening existing tourism corridors" (Garden Route, Cape Winelands, Overberg); (4) "Transport is primarily a connectivity and dispersal challenge, not a province-wide capacity crisis" with Cape Town International "the key gateway risk"; (5) "Thinner peripheral tourism economies remain structural constraints" (West Coast and Central Karoo) (p114). FA09, FA10, FA16, FA19.

### T21. Key messages (p114)

1. The Western Cape has the tourism assets to support growth, but readiness is uneven. "The constraint is less about creating new attractions and more about turning existing assets into complete, accessible visitor experiences."
2. Cape Town remains the engine of the system, but growth cannot keep concentrating there. "The Metro has the strongest tourism ecosystem, but congestion, peak pressure and concentration around established nodes make regional dispersal increasingly important."
3. The strongest regional opportunity lies in deepening existing tourism corridors. "The Garden Route, Cape Winelands and Overberg already have strong underlying product, but need better connectivity, packaging and service depth to convert that into longer stays and wider spend."
4. Transport is primarily a connectivity and dispersal challenge, not a province-wide capacity crisis. "Cape Town International is the key gateway risk, while George, rail and regional cruise nodes can play a stronger role in spreading visitors across the province."
5. Thinner peripheral tourism economies remain structural constraints. "The West Coast and Central Karoo show that differentiated assets alone are not enough when services, mobility and year-round demand remain weak."
