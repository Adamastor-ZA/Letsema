## Findings

1. The purpose of the benchmark is to identify structural tourism growth levers that enabled selected destinations to grow international tourism above global trends and to assess how they can be adapted to the Western Cape; it is "anchored in the hypothesis that doubling international arrivals by 2035 requires a shift from a volume-led, metro-centric tourism model to a value-driven, inclusive and system-oriented approach" (p6). Focus areas: all; framing for Chapter 2.3.

2. The document sets 3 to 5% YoY as the global tourism growth norm: "Global growth of 3–5% YoY reflects the historical norm under stable market conditions and post-recovery environments. This benchmark establishes the reference point for assessing the relative performance of comparator destinations" (p7). Figures: Organic Growth 3–5% YoY, "Long-term global baseline growth", "No structural acceleration" (p7); Strong Rebounds >5% YoY (multi-year), "Structural demand acceleration" (p7); Aggressive Rebounds 10%+ YoY, "Structural shift" (p7). Source cited: UN Tourism World Tourism Barometer, January 2025 (p7). Focus areas: context for all pillars; Chapter 2.3 baseline.

3. Regional 2024-2025 growth as stated on the slide: Global ~4% ("Global recovery reflects structural baseline demand, not rebound"); Europe ~4% ("mature and scale-led recovery"); Asia-Pacific ~6% ("continued reopening recovery"); Americas ~1%; Africa ~8% ("above global growth led by North Africa and selected SSA markets"); Middle East ~3% (p7). Closing line: "Above trend growth is rare at regional level, structural outperformance must be destination specific" (p7). Focus areas: 6 (Africa regional foundation), 4, 5.

4. Structural growth is defined as "sustained international tourism expansion above the global 3–5% reference range, driven by deliberate changes to demand drivers, access conditions or product systems, rather than cyclical recovery or short-term promotion" (p8). Three economic effects of levers: Structural Demand Creation (expands total addressable demand; "primary source of sustained above-trend growth"; examples new global positioning, new traveller segments, new travel occasions); Demand Activation and Acceleration (converts latent or constrained demand; "necessary to unlock growth but rarely sufficient alone"; examples route development, visa reform, targeted corridor marketing, air access improvements); System and Product Yield Optimisation (increases value per traveller "without increasing arrivals"; examples product densification, geographic dispersion, length-of-stay extension, premium curation) (p8). Focus areas: 1, 7, 10, 19 (classification used in Chapter 3 precedent lines).

5. The six comparators were chosen from a screen of 30: "We assessed and scored 30 global destinations across market, product, sustainability and performance criteria to identify those most comparable to the Western Cape" (p12). The scored list of 30 and their scores are not printed. Focus areas: method, Chapter 2.3.

6. The screen used 12 criteria in four groups (p11): Market & Access Dynamics (Long-Haul Dependence & Connectivity; Source Market Portfolio; Ease of Access & Visa), Destination & Product Structure (City–Region Configuration; Product & Experience Mix; Cost & Price Positioning), Growth, Inclusion & Sustainability (Growth Pattern & Tourism Development Stage; Inclusivity, Community Participation & SMME Landscape; Sustainability, Climate & Environmental Conditions), Performance & Competitiveness (Tourism Volume & Seasonality; Tourism Yield & Spend Levels; Airport & Tourism Infrastructure). The count "12" is not printed; it is the number of criterion columns. Source Market Portfolio names Wesgro's core and target markets as "UK/EU, North America, China, India, Middle East, Africa" (p11). Focus areas: method; 4, 5, 6.

7. The six selected comparators and the one-line rationale per case (p12): Chile ("A long-haul, city-gateway destination with strong coastal, wine and nature appeal. High comparability in dispersal challenges, product mix and long-haul reliance"); Auckland, New Zealand ("A dynamic center of culture, exploration, and scenic appeal, celebrated for its striking coastline and layered history. Urban life closely intertwined with natural landscapes"); Melbourne & Victoria ("Experience-rich region (wine, culinary, wildlife, touring). Strong long-haul dependence and multi-regional tourism footprint"); Valencia Region ("Coastal city with strong cultural and gastronomic appeal ... Comparable climate, product mix and price point"); Singapore ("Integrated approach to air access, service quality, food culture and visitor segmentation"); Kenya ("A gateway city linked to diverse tourism regions across coast, wildlife and culture. Strong alignment in long-haul reliance, regional dispersal and community-based tourism"). Focus areas: Chapter 2.3.

8. A single structured framework is applied to every case: 01 Destination Overview and Demand Profile (rationale for selection; snapshot of arrivals, spend, recovery depth, seasonality); 02 Proven Growth Levers ("Comprehensive view of two levers per country", including the primary structural lever and supporting supply-side enablers); 03 Transferability to the WC (demand impact; enabling conditions; relevance for Western Cape; required structural change locally) (p13). Each case also carries an "Institutional Coordination and Delivery System" slide laid out national/state-regional/local by public/private (p16, p23, p31, p38, p45, p52). Focus areas: 16, 17.

9. Summary growth rates per case, column headed "YoY Growth % Over 2023-2024" (p9): Chile ~+35%; Kenya ~12.8% - 14.6%; Singapore ~19%; Australia, Victoria Melbourne ~4,3%; New Zealand, Auckland Park ~12%; Spain, Valencia 13-14%. Recovery column "% Change Vs 2019 (Recovery Depth)": Chile ~82% recovered; Kenya ~102% recovered; Singapore "Slightly below, spend recovered"; Melbourne "Near full recovery, differentiation from yield, value density"; New Zealand "90% recovery, increased yield per visitor"; Valencia "124% recovered" (p9). Focus areas: Chapter 2.3 and Chapter 3 precedent lines.

10. Chile's primary lever is gateway-led air concentration: Santiago handles "~90–95% of ~5.2 million international arrivals" (p24); passenger traffic at SIA rose from ~16m (2010) to >26m (2019) (p25, p26); airport expansion raised capacity to 38m passengers (p25); international seat capacity grew ~40-50% between 2013 and 2019 (p26); Chile invested ~US$900 million in the Nuevo Pudahuel airport expansion via a private concession consortium, concession won in 2015 and awarded by the Chilean Ministry of Public Works (p26); investment included a new international terminal, apron and stand expansion, runway and taxiway improvements, expanded passenger processing and wide-body-capable gates (p26); new direct long-haul routes to London, Paris, Rome, Madrid, Barcelona, Melbourne, Sydney (p26). Chile "has one of the world's most liberal aviation systems, using open-skies agreements, strong aviation institutions, and private sector participation" (p23). Focus areas: 7, 15, 19.

11. Chile's demand profile: spend 2025 by region South America (55-60% of arrivals; Argentina, Brazil; Leisure/VFR) US$1.91bn; North America (15-18%; United States; Leisure) 575mn; Europe (12-15%; Spain, UK; Leisure) 480mn; Other (India, China, Aus; Leisure/Business) 320mn (p24). Purpose mix: Leisure/Vacation 65-70%, Business/MICE 7-10%, VFR 15-20% (p24). Recovery observation: "Recovery since 2022 shows demand rebounds strong when air capacity and destination appeal align" (p24). Focus areas: 4, 5, 2.

12. Chile's second lever is product diversification and immersive positioning (Santiago gateway, Valparaiso heritage-coastal hub, wine valleys as premium circuits, Patagonia as adventure flagship), with outcomes "Raised global awareness and monetised the shoulder season", "Increased length of stay as visitors explore multiple regions", "Created new demand segments focused on long-haul, high-yield travellers" (p27). Transferable lesson: "gateway cities, wine regions, coastal nodes and wilderness areas must operate as a designed portfolio, not standalone attractions" (p27). Chile's institutional challenges: "fragmented coordination across institutions, regional development imbalances beyond hubs like Santiago and limited formalisation of public–private collaboration" (p23). Focus areas: 10, 3, 16.

Focus areas: 7, 15, 19, 3, 11; Chapter 4.6 (Air Access Fund).

16. Kenya's first lever is conservation-led institutionalisation of wildlife, enabled by Kenya Wildlife Service, the Wildlife Conservation and Management Act (2013), National Wildlife Strategy 2030, a Community Conservancy Investment Model, public–private tourism investment frameworks and "Tourism-Funded Conservation Financing: Self-financing system that supports conservation and sustains tourism demand" (p33). Focus areas: 12, 13, 14, 15.

17. Kenya's second lever positions Nairobi as "East Africa's aviation, diplomatic, and business hub" with Kenya Airways' regional hub strategy, "significant investments in aviation infrastructure", diplomatic hub status (UNEP & UN-Habitat) and tourism policy coordination under Vision 2030 (p34). Institutional assessment: "Kenya's institutional framework has delivered meaningful reforms, however the effectiveness of collaboration between government, private sector and financiers is mixed. Required changes for WC include "Treat MICE as a system lever", "Align city, provincial and national actors around gateway strategy", "Formalise conservation and community inclusion as part of the tourism value proposition" (p35). 18. Singapore recorded "13 mn international visitor arrivals (19% increase from 2023), however still lagging pre-COVID"; "International tourism expenditure grew proportionately to $31.3 billion"; top market spend Mainland China $4.58 billion, Indonesia $2.89 billion, Australia $2 billion; "Tourist arrivals were spread fairly evenly across the year with peaks July-August" (all "Insights (2024)", p39). Focus areas: 4, 2, 5.

Focus areas: 4, 7, 16, 17.

Required change for WC: "Province-wide quality and standards framework that aligns all tourism stakeholders under consistent benchmarks led by Wesgro", "targeted investment in service excellence, work-force capability, and infrastructure", "embed continuous performance measurement and public–private accountability" (p42). 32. It cites the OECD on governance and destination management through partnership approaches, and the WEF TTDI 2024 on competitiveness as "a set of interconnected factors and policies" (p58). Key takeouts: "Tourism performance is systemic"; "Governance is what aligns the parts"; "Marketing converts strategic intent into demand"; "Accessibility convers demand into actual arrivals" (p58).
