## Findings

1. The assessment covers 50,332 tourism assets across six Western Cape regions, drawing on Wesgro, SANParks, StatsSA and CapeNature. Figures: 50,332 assets, 6 regions (p2, p3); "~50,332 tourism assets across the Western Cape's 6 district regions" (p4); "Consolidate 50,000 assets across six regions" (p17). Supports FA18, FA19.

2. The report positions itself under the "Transform Supply" pillar, whose objective is to "Remove mobility, infrastructure and product constraints to expand the destination's physical carrying capacity", and names six focus areas it addresses. Figures: 2 strategic focus areas (Accessibility, Connectivity and Infrastructure; Product and Destination Capacity) and 6 focus areas: Close the Air Access and Cruise Capacity Gaps; Accelerate Tourism-Enabling Internal Mobility; Lead Removal of Infrastructure, Visa and Safety Deficits; Support Diversified Route and Product Development; Align Visitor Experience In-Destination; Balance Impact on Nature-Based Attractions (p3). Supports FA07, FA08, FA09, FA10, FA11, FA12, FA19.

3. The report's framing is that "structural supply-side constraints, not a lack of demand, are the primary barrier" to growth. Figures: none numeric (p4); "Growth is supply-constrained, demand exists but infrastructure gaps prevent conversion" (p5). Supports FA19, FA15.

4. The executive summary names three established corridors as the structural backbone for visitor flow and states that infrastructure erodes with distance from Cape Town. Figures: 3 corridors (N2, R62, Winelands) (p5); "Infrastructure eroding rapidly with distance from Cape Town, weakening the visitor experience" (p5); "Over-reliance on the N2 spine and private car access that limits regional dispersal" (p5). Supports FA09, FA10, FA19.

5. The executive summary identifies shoulder-season events as "the single largest lever to flatten the demand curve" and events programming as "critically thin in shoulder and off-peak seasons". Figures: none numeric (p5). Supports FA02.

6. The executive summary states that a data layer is the prerequisite for targeted intervention. Figures: "Data and digital gaps undermining the ability to plan, market, and measure performance"; "A data layer is the prerequisite without it, no intervention can be targeted, measured and impactful" (p5). Supports FA18, FA03.

7. The supply system is modelled as three layers (Attractions, Services, Infrastructure) and destination performance is "constrained by the weakest layer". Figures: 3 layers; 12 categories; subtypes listed in the taxonomy table (p9, p10). Supports FA10, FA19.

8. The readiness model scores three dimensions on a common 1 to 5 scale with weights of 40/30/30. Figures: Attractiveness and Quality 40%; Readiness and Capacity 30%; Accessibility 30%; scale 1 Very weak, 2 Weak, 3 Functional, 4 Strong, 5 Leading (p17). Supports FA18.

9. Scores are explicitly relative and comparative, not absolute measures of destination quality, and missing data is not treated as poor performance. Figures: 4 stated assumptions (p18). Supports FA18.

10. Each touring route is assessed on four metrics and rated Ready, Developing or Gap on readiness dimensions; the slide says five dimensions but lists four (numbered 1, 2, 4, 5). Figures: metrics: From Cape Town (km), Route length (km), End-to-end drive (h), Suggested stay (nights); dimensions: Air and road access; Accommodation range; Safety and visitor support; Digital and bookability (p19). Supports FA10, FA03, FA19.

11. The Cape Metro handled about 11.1m two-way passengers through CTIA in 2025 and drives 72.5% of the province's GDPR. Figures: ~11.1m two-way passengers (3.3m international, 7.8m domestic; both +7% YoY); repeat visitation 45% domestic, 34% international; 72.5% of Western Cape GDPR (p22). Supports FA07, FA05.

12. The Cape Metro holds 20,281 assets, 40.3% of Western Cape supply, and leads every category share. Figures: 20,281 total (40.3% on the gauge; the slide subtitle rounds this to "~20,281 assets ... making 40% of Western Cape supply"); 6,563 accommodation (32.2%); 5,544 attractions (44.9%); 6,532 eateries (50.2%); 638 events (34.7%); 706 amenities (34.7%); percentages read from gauge charts (p23). "Leads every category share" is an inference from the four districts in these pages: the remaining two districts hold only 7,694 assets between them, so neither can exceed the Metro's share in any category. Supports FA10, FA19.

13. In the Metro the supply constraint is "not asset availability but spatial integration failure between cultural, coastal, and township tourism layers". Figures: primary corridor District Six, Atlantic Seaboard, Mountain-Peninsula spine; secondary cluster Constantia-Newlands and township culture and heritage nodes; cold zones industrial belts ("zero tourism conversion"), Cape Point and township attractions "underutilised despite existing attraction" (p24). Supports FA10, FA12, FA13.

14. Metro accommodation is "highly stratified and spatially concentrated", with townships "structurally excluded from formal accommodation ecosystems". Supports FA13, FA10.

15. The Metro has "world-class macro mobility infrastructure but fragmented micro-level tourist circuit integration". 17. Peak-season congestion weakens the Metro visitor experience, with Table Mountain Cableway queues of 2 to 3 hours and Chapman's Peak disruptions. Figures: 2-3 hours queue; most major attractions within approximately one hour of the airport (p28). Supports FA11, FA12.

## Definitions and method

- Three-layer supply system (p9): Attractions ("Why Travel": natural, cultural, heritage assets; guided tours, outdoor activities, events); Services ("What to do": accommodation, restaurants, bars, retail); Infrastructure ("How to access": airports, road networks, public services, utilities). "Destination performance is constrained by the weakest layer."
- Taxonomy (p10): 3 system layers, 12 categories, subtypes as tabulated above.
- Methodology (p11): three steps: 1 Asset inventory and classification (output: standardised tourism asset taxonomy); 2 Spatial mapping in GIS (output: asset maps by region; assessment of composition and maturity); 3 Readiness evaluation on three dimensions (output: asset scored; regional readiness score; gaps).
- Four-phase framework (p12 to p16): Phase 1 Tourism Asset Inventory and Readiness Assessment (Tourism Readiness Index; criteria: accessibility, service quality, service readiness, commercial viability, sustainability) (p13); Phase 2 Spatial Analysis, Infrastructure Mapping and Corridor Assessment in QGIS with Overpass dashboard, density and hotspot analyses (p14); Phase 3 Regional Tourism Profiling, Benchmarking and Readiness Assessment producing the Master Tourism Dataset and the Tourism Readiness and Attractiveness Scorecard; "Scores are calibrated against national and international benchmarks to produce a composite readiness rating" (p15; the assessment-interface image carries "xx" placeholders); Phase 4 Gap Analysis, Opportunity Assessment and Destination Readiness Strategy, classifying regions as market-ready and growth-ready, infrastructure constrained, or underutilised despite strong tourism assets (p16).
- Scoring rubric (p17): three dimensions weighted 40/30/30; indicators per dimension as tabulated; 1 to 5 scale; "Average indicator scores within each dimension, then combine dimensions using the agreed weights."
- Key assumptions (p18): 01 Asset volume does not equal readiness; 02 The same framework is applied across all regions; 03 Indicator scores are evidence informed, not purely formula derived ("some criteria require judgement informed by GIS analysis, desktop research and destination context"); 04 Missing data is not treated as poor performance. "A lower score does not mean a weak destination."
- Route scorecard method (p19): four metrics, four listed readiness dimensions (numbered 1, 2, 4, 5), three ratings.
- Regional assessment lenses (p20): Regional Role Setting; Tourism Asset Base Analysis; Spatial Mapping, Infrastructure Analysis and Route Assessment; Gap Analysis, Destination Readiness and Opportunity Assessment.
- Data sources cited (p4): Wesgro (primary supply database); SANParks (natural asset and conservation data); CapeNature (conservation reserves and biodiversity data); Overpass (geographical data points); WikiQuery (geographical data points); Tourism Reports 2023-2025 (geographical data points). The p2 scope line adds StatsSA.
- Dataset limits stated in these pages: only the assumptions on p18 (incomplete source data handled with best available evidence). No statement on sub-district geography or placeholder records appears in p1 to p57.
- Phase 1 inventory scope (p13, added in verification): five asset groups: attractions and experiences; accommodation establishments; eating and retail services; transport nodes and mobility infrastructure; tourism-support infrastructure.
- System logic (p9, added in verification): "The western cape can have world class assets, but poor access means people don't come, weak services mean people don't stay or spend"; "strong services and infrastructure with weak attractions will not generate enough demand for the region."
- Phase 3 benchmarking claim (p15): regions are "rated using a standardised scoring template that measures indicators such as infrastructure capacity, market accessibility, product diversity, and institutional support", calibrated "against national and international benchmarks". No benchmark values or comparator destinations are named anywhere in p1 to p57.
