## 0. The theme in December and in October

December 2025 theme 2.1 "Accessibility, Connectivity and Infrastructure" under pillar 2 TRANSFORM SUPPLY, objective "Remove mobility, infrastructure and product constraints to expand the destination's physical carrying capacity", held three entries: D7 Close the Air Access and Cruise Capacity Gaps; D8 Accelerate Tourism-Enabling Internal Mobility; D9 Lead Removal of Infrastructure, Visa and Safety Deficits (Current State Diagnostic, Dec 2025, p64; tree.md 1.1). The Infrastructure Report still lists all three among the six Transform Supply entries it addresses (Infrastructure Report, Jul 2026, p3).

October 2026 theme 2.1 holds FA07 Win direct routes and add seats on full routes (Reworded, from the air half of D7), FA08 Grow cruise calls and ports (New, from the cruise half of D7) and FA09 Accelerate tourism-enabling internal mobility (Validated, D8). D9 moved to theme 3.2 as FA19 (tree.md 2 and 3, changes 1, 2 and 7). The split of D7 is the only structural change inside this theme; the deliverable rationale for it is that MIDAS gave air access its own evidence base (route loads, hub connections, fares before seats) while the Infrastructure Report's cruise section concluded that cruise runs at about 40% of berth capacity and is a product-and-marketing lever, a different problem from airport capacity (tree.md 3, change 1; Infrastructure Report, Jul 2026, p110 to p112).

The Infrastructure Report's own framing for the theme: "The airport, not a shortage of visitors is what now caps Cape Town's tourism growth" (p101); "Transport is primarily a connectivity and dispersal challenge, not a province-wide capacity crisis", with "Cape Town International is the key gateway risk, while George, rail and regional cruise nodes can play a stronger role in spreading visitors across the province" (p114, key message 4).

### (d) Benchmark precedent

Two cases in the Benchmarking Report are direct precedents.

Chile, gateway-led air connectivity (Global Benchmarking Report, Mar 2026, p25, p26, p28). Chile invested ~US$900 million in the Nuevo Pudahuel expansion through a private concession won in 2015 and awarded by the Ministry of Public Works, lifting capacity to 38m passengers, growing international seats ~40-50% between 2013 and 2019 and opening direct long-haul routes to London, Paris, Rome, Madrid, Barcelona, Melbourne and Sydney, under "one of the world's most liberal aviation systems, using open-skies agreements" (p23). The transferable change for the Western Cape is to "Treat air access as core economic infrastructure, not a tourism add-on", "Establish a permanent, well-funded Public–Private Air Access Fund" and "Actively lobby for air service liberalisation and simplified visa regimes" (p28).

New Zealand, demand-engineered air access (p54, p56). New Zealand "shifted from awareness-driven marketing to route accountable demand generation", tying marketing budgets to specific long-haul corridors and airline breakeven thresholds, with "16,000+ incremental bookings" and "(~NZD 55m ROI, 2023/24)" as the printed outcomes, enabled by "Government backed early-stage route incentives" and "Shared data platforms between tourism bodies and airlines". The required change is to "Ring-fence budgets around priority long-haul corridors", "Formalise an Aviation Partnership Framework" and "Introduce structured early-stage route support mechanisms" (p56).

Supporting precedents: Singapore's stopover demand conversion by STB, Changi Airport Group and Singapore Airlines "under a coordinated strategy" (p40), with CAAS negotiating bilateral air service agreements (p38); Melbourne "uses its event calendar to de-risk and stimulate aviation demand. Accessibility is pulled by demand, not just enabled by supply" (p59). MIDAS's own peer set is a working comparison inside the deliverable: Cape Town's 17 mainline routes added 2015 to 2025 lead Santiago, Valencia and Calgary, and "Calgary has the closest profile to Capetown in terms of the mix of international and domestic capacity" ("International Comparisons").

### (f) What the deliverables recommend

What Wesgro can do:
- Pursue "tactical opportunities ... for upgrades in frequency development in some markets and potentially aircraft up gauging could be encouraged from some airlines" (MIDAS Aviation Market Assessment, Mar 2026, "In Conclusion"); consider "further capacity increases" on Luanda, Maputo, Gaborone, Harare and Mauritius ("Seasonality").
- "Secure additional frequency growth from Emirates, Qatar and Ethiopian Airlines"; "develop long-term route cases for India and China corridors"; "expand Cape Town Air Access partnership activity with coordinated airline incentives and market support" (Demand-Side Analysis, Jun 2026, p54; same list on Market Assessment Report, May 2026, p58 [working analysis]).
- "develop and maintain a relationship between WESGRO and both Indian carriers and the respective Government authorities in India" ("The Indian Market Opportunity"); focus on "the other state-owned entities of China Southern or China Eastern", "build confidence in the seasonality of the market on a year-round basis" and treat China as "long-term investment where increasing awareness leading to more indirect travel volumes needs to be implemented before any non-stop service could be considered" ("The Chinese Market Opportunity"); begin "to engage with the airline management team in Riyadh Air over the coming years" ("The Saudi Arabian Market Opportunity").
- "Treat runway delivery as the #1 risk; track approvals and milestones quarterly"; "commission a capacity-vs-demand model to confirm the timeline clears 2035" (Infrastructure Report, Jul 2026, p109; p103).
- George: "Monitor weather reliability and seat growth; no capital ask"; press ACSA and ATNS on landing-system reliability (p105, p109). Cape Winelands: "Enable road/airspace; watch airline and funding commitments; 'Don't bank it'" (p109); keep CTIA's expansion as the primary plan (p107). Plettenberg Bay "Light watching brief"; Beaufort West "Do nothing further" (p108).
- Scenario framing to carry into the plan: the Base case assumes "Emirates, Qatar, Ethiopian and Turkish Airlines continue adding frequency and capacity into Cape Town"; the Accelerated case assumes "Direct India and China routes secured" (Demand-Side Analysis, Jun 2026, p19).

What partners must do:
- ACSA: deliver the ~R11.3bn CTIA programme (runway builder appointed Dec 2026, construction from ~2027, runway done ~2033, international terminal ~2032, domestic terminal ~2034) and the R310m George terminal by ~2028 with the ~2027 landing-system upgrade (Infrastructure Report, Jul 2026, p103, p105). ATNS: landing-system approvals at George after the January 2025 lapse that cancelled 10+ flights in a day (p104).
- Cape Winelands Airport (private, Growthpoint equity partner): close the ~R8bn funding and name a carrier; the province and roads authority: upgrade R302 / R312 / R304 for 2.5m+ passengers; ACSA and the airport: airspace coordination for two airports ~30km apart (p106 to p107).
- Airlines: frequency and gauge on full routes (MIDAS "In Conclusion"); hub carriers' Cape Town capacity (Demand-Side p20).
- Transport Network Port Authority is not an air-access partner; see FA08.

What sits nationally and must be escalated:
- "Air service agreements and route rights" and "Visa rules and border compliance" are national-tier functions (Current State Diagnostic, Dec 2025, p58); "US carriers are capped, and South Africa's reluctance to sign Open Skies is constraining one of Cape Town's best-yielding markets" (Inception Report, Nov 2025, p8, project-team synthesis); "Restrictive bilateral air agreements" (Stakeholder Insights Report, Mar 2026, p24, High).
- Chinese international routes are "frequently an extension of government-to-government relations with routes being launched as part of international trade agreements" (MIDAS, "The Chinese Market Opportunity").
- "improve immigration processing and peak-hour airport throughput at Cape Town International" (Demand-Side Analysis, Jun 2026, p54) sits with Home Affairs and ACSA; "limited immigration counters" (Inception Report, Nov 2025, p8).
- Benchmark instruction: "Actively lobby for air service liberalisation and simplified visa regimes" (Global Benchmarking Report, Mar 2026, p28). Cross-reference FA19 and FA16.

### (d) Benchmark precedent

None in the Benchmarking Report. None of the six cases (Chile, Kenya, Singapore, Melbourne, Valencia, New Zealand) carries a cruise lever, port figure or home-porting mechanism, and the final screening criteria replaced the Inception's "Cruise & Multi-Modal Access" criterion (Inception Report, Nov 2025, p30) with "Ease of Access & Visa" (Global Benchmarking Report, Mar 2026, p11). Vancouver, described at inception as having "a major cruise port" (Inception Report, Nov 2025, p32), was dropped from the final six; Athens, Dubrovnik and Seattle were screened out partly because they are "cruise-heavy" or "cruise-centric" (Current State Diagnostic, Dec 2025, p61; Inception Report, Nov 2025, p31).

Nearest case in the Benchmarking Report: Singapore's conversion of transit traffic into destination visits through the Singapore Stopover Programme and Free Singapore Tour, run by STB, Changi Airport Group and Singapore Airlines "under a coordinated strategy" (Global Benchmarking Report, Mar 2026, p40), which is the structural analogue for turning calls and turnarounds into shore spend and pre- and post-cruise stays; and Chile's positioning of Valparaiso as a "heritage-coastal hub" inside a designed regional portfolio (p27). Both are analogies, not cruise precedents, and should be labelled as such.

### (f) What the deliverables recommend

What Wesgro can do:
- "grow it through product and marketing, not capital works" (Infrastructure Report, Jul 2026, p112). Owners and next 12 months as printed: Cape Town "weather and berth scheduling (TNPA)"; Mossel Bay "excursion product and line marketing"; Saldanha "prove demand before capital" (p112). Priorities: Cape Town Protect; Mossel Bay Back now; Saldanha Bay Watch.
- Extend itineraries west and east from the Cape Town anchor: "Cape Town anchors the province; the regional ports extend itineraries west and east"; Mossel Bay and Saldanha "are day-stop opportunities rather than home-ports" (p111).
- Package excursions professionally, the lever Mossel Bay credits for its growth (Stakeholder Insights Report, Mar 2026, p20), and put community product on cruise-operator channels (SME and Community Inclusion Plan, Sep 2026, p56, Knysna northern townships first move).
- Develop home porting and expedition segments with SADC partners (Inception Report, Nov 2025, p9, project-team synthesis), through the Cruise Cape Town unit (p7).
- Track "cruise volumes" as an M&E indicator (Inception Report, Nov 2025, p23).

What partners must do:
- Transnet National Ports Authority: berth scheduling and weather management at Cape Town, wind mitigation in the 2026–31 plan, shore power (none today), the 300m ship-length ceiling (Infrastructure Report, Jul 2026, p110, p112).
- Mossel Bay municipality and tourism office: run the R22m reception facility, excursion product and line marketing (p111, p112); safety management for cruise arrivals (Stakeholder Insights Report, Mar 2026, p20).
- Saldanha Bay municipality and Transnet: demonstrate demand before any cruise berth; a passenger facility is absent (Infrastructure Report, Jul 2026, p111, p112, p65).
- V&A Waterfront (terminal concessionaire) and the cruise lines (MSC, Cunard, Norwegian, Regent): line-up and turnaround product (p110).

What sits nationally:
- Port investment priorities are Transnet's: Cape Town's container expansion (Dec 2026) and Saldanha's R4bn iron-ore expansion (Sept 2026) carry "zero cruise" (Infrastructure Report, Jul 2026, p112); the Port of Cape Town's "congestion and equipment failures" are a national SOE matter (Current State Diagnostic, Dec 2025, p34). No deliverable proposes a second berth or asks the province to fund one.

### (d) Benchmark precedent

None in the Benchmarking Report on internal mobility as such: no case carries a public-transport, shuttle, rail-for-tourism or last-mile lever with figures. Nearest cases:

- Chile, hub-and-spoke dispersal (Global Benchmarking Report, Mar 2026, p25, p27): "Strong domestic air network connects Santiago to secondary tourist regions such as Patagonia, Atacama, Valparaiso and the wine valleys", and the lesson that "gateway cities, wine regions, coastal nodes and wilderness areas must operate as a designed portfolio, not standalone attractions". The transfer is the designed-portfolio logic; the Western Cape's secondary gateway is George, not a domestic air network.
- Singapore, the integrated gateway (p40, p60): "Continuous investment in Changi Airport ... digital airport services and world-class passenger amenities" and a system that "integrates aviation, infrastructure and visitor experience into a single seamless gateway"; the analogue for airport-to-city movement and an airport rail link, which Cape Town lacks (Infrastructure Report, Jul 2026, p100).
- New Zealand, regional circuits (p51, p55): the tourism chain runs Access, Conversion, Value, Dispersal, Resilience; "Regional clusters must shape premium ready circuits not rely on Cape Town alone". The precedent for packaging corridors, silent on the transport that moves visitors along them.
- Melbourne's event infrastructure lists "integrated transport connectivity" as a supply enabler (p18), one line, no figures.

Outside the Benchmarking Report, the only mobility precedent with costs and volumes in hand is Medellín's Comuna 13 in the Inclusion plan (SME and Community Inclusion Plan, Sep 2026, p8), where city mobility infrastructure "built for residents became the stage for a resident product", with the caution that governance and a 667-visitors-a-day carrying capacity came late. In the stakeholder record the Department of Mobility offers two home-grown models: the George Integrated Public Transport Network, which "indicates that affordable, reliable and safe local public transport can support tourism demand in secondary destinations" (Stakeholder Insights Report, Mar 2026, p22, Medium), and "a dedicated transport authority model" (p23, High).

### (f) What the deliverables recommend

What Wesgro can do:
- Build a tourism product on the rail that already runs: the Southern Line is "a ready-made, low-cost scenic corridor" whose visitor branding "hasn't been refreshed" since 2007; "A reliable rail spine could disperse visitors beyond the CBD and Atlantic Seaboard without adding to road congestion" (Infrastructure Report, Jul 2026, p100).
- Route-level opportunities printed on the scorecards: Cape Peninsula "Strengthen public and tourism transport connectivity along the Peninsula corridor" (p30); Cape Winelands "Improve estate-to-estate and town-to-town mobility through integrated transport solutions" (p51); Garden Route "Position George as a stronger tourism gateway through improved air connectivity and visitor services" (p40); West Coast "shuttle services, EV charging infrastructure and improved visitor information" (p60).
- "Improve regional mobility and tourism infrastructure to support geographic dispersal" (Demand-Side Analysis, Jun 2026, p56; Market Assessment Report, May 2026, p60 [working analysis]).
- Aggregate demand for shuttles and integrate booking (Stakeholder Insights Report, Mar 2026, p41, !Khwa ttu); organised transport such as the seasonal City Sightseeing services "can unlock additional demand" (p27, Overberg).
- Position Cape Town "as a 'high-quality launchpad' that seamlessly connects tourists into secondary districts rather than holding them in the city bowl" (Current State Diagnostic, Dec 2025, p41).
- Address access "from the community side": the Inclusion plan's pathway "Physical access and mobility: Address last-mile arrival, movement and departure from the community side" (SME and Community Inclusion Plan, Sep 2026, p37) and its four launch gates, arrival, defined route, visitor activity, departure (p35).

What partners must do:
- PRASA and the City of Cape Town: Central Line recovery (~70% complete; Philippi–Kapteinsklip targeted Jan 2026), the R21.1bn fleet and infrastructure programme, tap-and-go ticketing, station security, and the devolution case (Infrastructure Report, Jul 2026, p100); MyCiTi Phase 2 (R3.3bn; Phase 2A stations Sep 2026, buses late 2027 per the Inclusion plan) and the unfunded last mile to Chapman's Peak, Constantia and Cape Point (p32).
- Department of Mobility: the Cape Town–George rapid rail assessment, the George Integrated Public Transport Network as a model, integrated ticketing, taxi and e-hailing supply and safety, and the case for "a dedicated transport authority model" (Stakeholder Insights Report, Mar 2026, p22, p23); the Diagnostic's four enablers (Current State Diagnostic, Dec 2025, p38).
- Municipalities and the provincial roads authority: R17m Malgas road re-gravelling (25km); R62 reconstruction (Ashton–Cogmans Kloof–Montagu) underway; Swartberg Pass and secondary access "unbudgeted"; rural roads limiting access to inland towns; R302 / R312 / R304 for the Cape Winelands Airport (Infrastructure Report, Jul 2026, p76, p87, p107).
- Private operators: the Franschhoek Wine Tram model, heritage rail (Outeniqua Choo Tjoe potential, Ceres Rail, Rovos Rail), City Sightseeing, e-hailing coverage in Darling and similar nodes (p53, p38, p100; Stakeholder Insights Report, Mar 2026, p27, p41).
- Funding: "climate, development, blended-finance and private-sector funding models" for tourism-enabling mobility (Stakeholder Insights Report, Mar 2026, p23, High).

What sits nationally:
- Passenger rail is PRASA's: R1.9tn national infrastructure plan over 25 years with Cape Town Metrorail the second priority, and "no public rail investment beyond the Cape Metro" (Infrastructure Report, Jul 2026, p100).
- "National rules preventing bank-card payments on the George bus service" (Stakeholder Insights Report, Mar 2026, p23, Medium).
- "Mobility responsibilities are fragmented across local, provincial and national government" (p23, High); the national road network (SANRAL N1 dual carriageway about R700m, SME and Community Inclusion Plan, Sep 2026, p26).
