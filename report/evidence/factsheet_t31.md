### (d) Benchmark precedent

**In the Benchmarking Report.** Kenya, lever 1 (Global Benchmarking Report, Mar 2026, p33, p35; institutions p31). Kenya "institutionalised conservation as national economic infrastructure, embedding wildlife protection, tourism development, and community participation into a coordinated governance and investment system", with a "Community Conservancy Investment Model", "public–private tourism investment frameworks" and "Tourism-Funded Conservation Financing: Self-financing system that supports conservation and sustains tourism demand" (p33); outcomes listed include "Benefits across rural communities" (p33). Required change for the Western Cape: "formalise conservation and community inclusion as part of the tourism value proposition" and "strengthen coordination across public & private sector and communities" (p35); the institutional slide names Community Based Organisations as "Locally managed wildlife conservancies and community tourism projects" (p31). Benchmark tier; no community spend or job figure is printed for Kenya.

**Second precedent.** Valencia, lever 2 (p48, p49). Resident-embedded planning: "The city includes citizens in the development initiative, ensuring the diversification strategy involves the community and aligns with their needs" (p48); required change "Integrate residents formally into planning forums" and, under lever 1, "Reduce SMME burden via subsidies and capability support" and "Make certification visible across booking platforms and brand communication" (p49). Benchmark tier.

**The deliverable's own cases** (SME and Community Inclusion Plan, Sep 2026, p7 to p11), which are the precedents the report should lead with because they carry figures: Rio Top Tour, first phase about R$230,000 (R$184,000 federal, R$46,000 state), about 20 resident guides by 2015, security collapsed 2017 (p7); Medellín Comuna 13, visitors 1.66 million in 2022 and 1.98 million in 2025, 52% international, more than 1,000 businesses and about 300 professional guides, 2023 carrying-capacity study estimating 667 visitors per day (p8); Vilakazi Street, Sakhumzi from four employees (2001) to about 110 (2019), ten restaurants more than 800 jobs, 13 guesthouses formed an association (p9); Costa Rica ACTUAR, turnover more than USD 800,000 in 2013 for more than 30 member communities, dissolved end of 2018 (p10); Kliptown, Walter Sisulu Square R160 million to R180 million built before an operator, about 4,000 museum visitors a year by 2017 against a business case of almost 200,000 international visitors a year, local small businesses 5% of construction contract value (p11).

### (f) What the deliverables recommend

**The Inclusion Plan's own response lines** (SME and Community Inclusion Plan, Sep 2026): the core model is three parts, "1 functioning selling capability", "2 connection to existing demand", "3 activation and investment gates", with "PILOT 2–3 OF 6" selected on readiness, tested, measured and scaled "after thresholds are met" (p32); the first deliverable of selling capability is "One live booking route, published operating dates, a named customer contact and a simple record of bookings and local revenue" (p33); demand connection runs through "itinerary or booking channel, accommodation package, event programme, established visitor experience" on "Commission, fees, revenue share and payment arrangements" (p34); four launch gates (arrival, defined route, visitor activity, departure) with "SAPS station totals as context only" and "A formal safety assessment remains a separate launch requirement" (p35); common sequence "1 validate offer, 2 establish booking route, 3 complete safety plan, 4 test on fixed date, 5 invest after demand, 6 scale through channels" (p31); the six response pathways map to the three pillars (digital visibility and itinerary integration; events in the community; catalytic asset; physical access and mobility; procurement integration; organisation and skills) with "Safety and access apply across all pathways and must be addressed before activation" (p37); the plan's own framing step is "what Wesgro can move" (p3).

Chile invested "~US$900 million in the Nuevo Pudahuel airport expansion via a private concession consortium", concession won in 2015 and awarded by the Ministry of Public Works, with international seat capacity up "~40-50% between 2013 and 2019" and new direct long-haul routes to London, Paris, Rome, Madrid, Barcelona, Melbourne and Sydney. Required change for the Western Cape: "Treat air access as core economic infrastructure, not a tourism add-on" and "Establish a permanent, well-funded Public–Private Air Access Fund" (p28); the fund carries no amount.

**Second precedent.** New Zealand, lever 1 (p54, p56). "Route accountable demand generation" produced "16,000+ incremental bookings" and "(~NZD 55m ROI, 2023/24)", enabled by "Government backed early-stage route incentives", "Shared data platforms between tourism bodies and airlines" and "multi-stakeholder co-investment tied to route-level KPIs"; required change "Ring-fence budgets around priority long-haul corridors", "Formalise an Aviation Partnership Framework", "Introduce structured early-stage route support mechanisms" (p56). Lever 2 adds the capital-discipline rule "Infrastructure Scaled to Premium ('Avoid high-volume build out that erodes pricing power')" (p55).

**Supporting cases.** Melbourne's event-led demand pulled "+21% Hotel room growth", "5000 new rooms" and "Entry global hotel brands" (p18). Singapore's quality uplift used the Hotel Rejuvenation Fund, Tourism Development Fund, Business Improvement Fund and LEAD programme (p41; no amounts). Kenya's "public–private tourism investment frameworks" and "Community Conservancy Investment Model" (p33). All benchmark tier. No comparator slide gives a destination-management budget, a tourism levy or a blended-finance structure, so the stakeholder proposals in 15.10 have no benchmark precedent in the report.
