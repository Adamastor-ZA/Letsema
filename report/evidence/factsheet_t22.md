### (d) Benchmark precedent

Available in the Benchmarking Report, two cases:

- Chile, lever 2, product diversification and immersive positioning (Global Benchmarking Report, Mar 2026, p27; required change p28). Santiago as gateway, Valparaíso as heritage-coastal hub, the wine valleys as premium circuits and Patagonia as adventure flagship were run as one portfolio, with the printed outcomes "Raised global awareness and monetised the shoulder season", "Increased length of stay as visitors explore multiple regions" and "Created new demand segments focused on long-haul, high-yield travellers". The transferable lesson is that "gateway cities, wine regions, coastal nodes and wilderness areas must operate as a designed portfolio, not standalone attractions", and the required change for the Western Cape is to "Sell experiences as curated journeys, move from asset marketing to experience led packaging" and "Unify destination narrative across province, city, and sectors".
- New Zealand, lever 2, regional value engineering (Global Benchmarking Report, Mar 2026, p55; required change p56; comparability p51). Premium product was curated into multi-day itineraries with capacity discipline, so that "Regional product curation must be deliberate, not incidental" and "Regional clusters must shape premium ready circuits not rely on Cape Town alone". The required change is "deliberate regional product curation frameworks" and to "shape premium-ready regional circuits beyond Cape Town core", with the reference point "Use Cape Town as a gateway: To spread value provincially through bookable regional experiences".

Nearest supporting case: Kenya lever 2, whose Western Cape relevance is "a critical shift from a Cape Town-centric leisure model to a multi-functional gateway system" and "Support multi-region tourism circuits across the country" (p34). Cross-cutting sub-theme: "from destination to journey (multi-node experience)" (p61). No benchmark case prints a route-readiness score or an asset count.

### (d) Benchmark precedent

Available in the Benchmarking Report, two cases, both strong:

- Singapore, lever 2, institutional quality uplift of the visitor economy (Global Benchmarking Report, Mar 2026, p41; required change p42). The Singapore Tourism Board worked with the Singapore Hotel Association through the Hotel Industry Transformation Map, a government Hotel Rejuvenation Fund, the Hotels Act, the NEA Hawker Management System and the Tourism Development Fund, Business Improvement Fund and LEAD programme, with printed outcomes of high visitor trust, increased average spend, longer stays and a shift "from lower-value stopover visits toward longer stays and higher-value tourism spending". The required change for the Western Cape is a "Province-wide quality and standards framework that aligns all tourism stakeholders under consistent benchmarks led by Wesgro", with "targeted investment in service excellence, work-force capability, and infrastructure" and "continuous performance measurement and public–private accountability".
- Valencia, lever 1, destination-wide quality and sustainability standardisation (Global Benchmarking Report, Mar 2026, p47; required change p49). Turisme Comunitat Valenciana ran the Qualitur Programme and Club and adopted SICTED as the destination quality system, with outcomes of improved consistency and reliability of services, stronger reputation for quality and higher repeat visitation. The required changes are to "Establish a provincial-level quality authority or charter", "Phase implementation by district but embed in provincial policy", "Reduce SMME burden via subsidies and capability support", "Make certification visible across booking platforms and brand communication" and move "from 'encouraged compliance' to institutionalised compliance".

Nearest case for the supply side of the mid-scale gap: Melbourne's event-led lever was backed by "+21% Hotel room growth", "5000 new rooms" and the entry of global hotel brands (p18), the only room-count precedent in the report.

### (f) What the deliverables recommend

Wesgro can do:

- Lead a province-wide quality and standards framework "under consistent benchmarks led by Wesgro" (Global Benchmarking Report, Mar 2026, p42) and, in Valencia's form, "a provincial-level quality authority or charter" with certification "visible across booking platforms" (p49). This is the Benchmarking Report's own assignment of the role to Wesgro; no interim deliverable assigns it.
- Destination readiness for growth markets: "multilingual support, digital payment compatibility and culturally relevant experiences" and "market-specific itineraries and packaged experiences" (Demand-Side Analysis, Jun 2026, p55; Market Assessment Report, May 2026, p59, working analysis).
- Peninsula route: "Shift from attraction development to destination optimisation" (Infrastructure Report, Jul 2026, p30); Route 62: "Strengthen interpretation and storytelling around heritage, culture and landscapes" (p84); Garden Route: "Develop cultural and heritage experiences to complement the route's dominant nature offering" (p40). Cape Winelands route opportunity 1 reads "Shift from destination marketing to ecosystem development" (p51, verbatim; banned term, paraphrase in the body).
- Measurement: "embed continuous performance measurement and public–private accountability to close the gap between destination promise and on-the-ground delivery" (Global Benchmarking Report, Mar 2026, p42); Skift's quarterly digital-sentiment tracking as the only in-hand visitor-perception baseline (Skift Digital memo, 10 Dec 2025, section 6 "Next Steps" [p4]).

Partners must do:

- Municipalities: pedestrianisation and public realm in Stellenbosch and Hermanus (Stakeholder Insights Report, Mar 2026, p22, Medium); municipal services as a prerequisite, Mossel Bay (p20, High); Hermanus peak-season roads and parking strain (Infrastructure Report, Jul 2026, p75).
- Private sector and investors: mid-scale and branded conference-capable hotel supply in secondary nodes (Stakeholder p21 Mossel Bay; Infrastructure p95); accommodation depth east of Hermanus, across Route 62's secondary towns and beyond Langebaan and Paternoster (Infrastructure p73, p84, p62). The Infrastructure Report lists no committed capital for any of these (p65, p76, p87).
- Attraction operators: Robben Island interpretation and product refresh (Stakeholder p32); Cableway lower-station activations (p36); Cape Nature service readiness and tiered pricing (p43, p44); CTICC off-peak programming (p45).
- Cape Nature and training partners: upskilling in communities around reserves before "consistent customer service standards can be met" (p43), carried to FA20.
- Valencia's supporting measure, "Reduce SMME burden via subsidies and capability support" (Global Benchmarking Report, Mar 2026, p49), would sit with DEDAT and municipal support programmes; the deliverable does not name the South African actor.

Sits nationally:

- Arrival experience at CTIA: "Improve immigration processing and peak-hour airport throughput at Cape Town International" (Demand-Side Analysis, Jun 2026, p54; Market Assessment Report, May 2026, p58); the domestic arrivals hall "Never rebuilt in the 2010 upgrade, chronically undersized" and the international terminal "Arrivals and departures badly congested at peak hours", relief from ~2032 under ACSA's ~R11.3bn programme (Infrastructure Report, Jul 2026, p101 to p103). ACSA is national; immigration processing is a national function.
- Rural connectivity and payments: SA Connect (Infrastructure Report, Jul 2026, p65, p87); "Poor connectivity has also delayed cashless operations" at remote reserves (Stakeholder Insights Report, Mar 2026, p43).
- Cape Nature's "statutory affordability mandate" that keeps pricing conservative (Stakeholder Insights Report, Mar 2026, p44, Medium) is a statutory setting outside Wesgro's reach; the deliverable does not say which tier of government holds it.

### (d) Benchmark precedent

Available in the Benchmarking Report, two cases:

- Kenya, lever 1, conservation-led institutionalisation of wildlife (Global Benchmarking Report, Mar 2026, p33; required change p35). Kenya "institutionalised conservation as national economic infrastructure" through the Kenya Wildlife Service, the Wildlife Conservation and Management Act (2013), National Wildlife Strategy 2030, a Community Conservancy Investment Model and "Tourism-Funded Conservation Financing: Self-financing system that supports conservation and sustains tourism demand", with outcomes that include high-value international travellers, benefits across rural communities, smoothed seasonality and maintained environmental integrity. The required change is to "Formalise conservation and community inclusion as part of the tourism value proposition" and "shift from asset-led to system-led tourism development".
- New Zealand, lever 2, regional value engineering through strategic de-commoditisation (Global Benchmarking Report, Mar 2026, p55; required change p56). Controlled capacity management via land-use controls, premium curation and conservation as an economic lever produced "Increased revenue per visitor", "Stronger alignment between conservation and economic return" and "Lower substitutability vs. other scenic destinations", on the principle that "Capacity discipline protects long-term pricing power" and "Avoid high-volume build out that erodes pricing power". The required change is to "replace arrivals KPI with yield-based metrics" and "align conservation and premium access to protect pricing power".

No benchmark case prints a visitor cap or a carrying-capacity method; the only numeric carrying-capacity precedent in hand is Comuna 13's 667 visitors a day (SME and Community Inclusion Plan, Sep 2026, p8), which is a township corridor, not a nature site.

### (f) What the deliverables recommend

Wesgro can do:

- Steer demand away from constrained flagships: "directing demand toward under-visited regions and regional itineraries" (Stakeholder Insights Report, Mar 2026, p8); market the "overflow reserves [that] remain under-marketed" (Infrastructure Report, Jul 2026, p88); "Improve visitor dispersal beyond the highest-volume attractions" and "Expand shoulder-season experiences to reduce peak-period pressure" (p30).
- Replace arrivals with yield metrics for nature product: "Replace arrival growth with yield-based regional performance metrics" (Global Benchmarking Report, Mar 2026, p55) and "replace arrivals KPI with yield-based metrics" (p56); the Department of Economic Development's own "a value/spend lens may be more appropriate than arrivals" (Stakeholder Insights Report, Mar 2026, p24, High).
- Position conservation as the proposition: "Position conservation and biodiversity as core differentiators" (Whale Coast route, Infrastructure Report, Jul 2026, p73); "Formalise conservation and community inclusion as part of the tourism value proposition" (Global Benchmarking Report, Mar 2026, p35).
- Use the Southern Line as a tourism product to reach Boulders and Simon's Town without road congestion: "a ready-made, low-cost scenic corridor" whose branding dates to 2007 (Infrastructure Report, Jul 2026, p100). The rail asset is PRASA's; the product and marketing are not.

Partners must do:

- Attraction and reserve managers (SANParks, Table Mountain Aerial Cableway, Cape Nature, Robben Island Museum, City of Cape Town): visitor-flow management, "Digital solutions to better manage visitor flows and optimise demand at high-traffic attractions" (Demand-Side Analysis, Jun 2026, p3); "implement visitor flow management in sensitive areas" (Skift Segmentation Analysis, Mar 2026, 5. Destination Management Implications [p25], working analysis); coordinated action on Table Mountain "across the city, tourism bodies, the operator and conservation stakeholders" (Stakeholder Insights Report, Mar 2026, p36, High).
- Cape Nature: climate-resilient product, "reassessing asset formats and siting" (p43); tiered pricing (p44); off-grid solar and self-sufficiency at remote sites (p44, Medium).
- Robben Island Museum: product diversification across the island's broader assets and exhibition-led interpretation (p32).
- Municipalities: Hermanus peak-season roads and parking (Infrastructure Report, Jul 2026, p75); Overstrand's Whale Festival operations and community buy-in (Stakeholder p26).
- PRASA and City of Cape Town: Southern Line reliability and station security, "not an explicit line item" in current plans (Infrastructure Report, Jul 2026, p100); "Existing MyCiTi services are not being adopted by tourists" at Table Mountain (Stakeholder p36).

Sits nationally:

- Table Mountain National Park, Cape of Good Hope (Cape Point) and Boulders sit with SANParks, a national entity (the Inception Report lists South African National Parks among key external stakeholders, Nov 2025, p7); the Cableway reports that "Environmental restrictions and strong public sensitivity around Table Mountain severely limit permanent infrastructure expansion on the summit" (Stakeholder Insights Report, Mar 2026, p36). The deliverables do not say which tier sets the restriction.
- Conservation funding: Cape Nature "cannot rely on public funding alone" (p44); tourism-funded conservation financing is the Kenyan precedent (Global Benchmarking Report, Mar 2026, p33). The funding decision sits outside Wesgro; the deliverables do not say which tier would take it. Cape Nature's "statutory affordability mandate" (p44) is likewise a statutory setting the deliverable does not locate by tier.
- PRASA's R21.1bn renewal and the R1.9tn national plan carry the Southern Line (Infrastructure Report, Jul 2026, p100), national.
