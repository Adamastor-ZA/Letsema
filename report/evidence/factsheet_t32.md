### (d) Benchmark precedent

**In the Benchmarking Report.** Every case carries an "Institutional Coordination and Delivery System" slide laid out national, state or regional and local by public and private (Global Benchmarking Report, Mar 2026, p16, p23, p31, p38, p45, p52). Two sentences per relevant case:

- New Zealand (p51, p52). Governance works because "policy, marketing, conservation, and regional tourism development are clearly separated but coordinated across national, regional, and local levels, while the private sector drives tourism product delivery" (p52). The deck warns that "NZ's national governance context differs; transfer requires adapting governance, data and capability to WC realities" (p51).
- Valencia (p45, p47, p49). Coordination runs through a permanent public–private structure led by Visit València, "embedding municipal, provincial and regional authorities into a single decision-making system" (p47; source sentence opens with a "formalised" variant, so quote from "embedding"). The required change for the Western Cape is to "Embed tourism governance into permanent public–private structures" (p49).
- Victoria and Melbourne (p16). The coordination slide records "Ongoing tension and need for clarity between national and state tourism bodies over roles and branding remains a concern". It is the closest analogue to the SA Tourism and Wesgro relationship and shows the problem is not unique to the Western Cape.
- Kenya (p31, p35). "Kenya's institutional framework has delivered meaningful reforms, however the effectiveness of collaboration between government, private sector and financiers is mixed" (p31). Required change: "Align city, provincial and national actors around gateway strategy" (p35).
- Air rights in every comparator sit with a national body: Chile's Junta Aeronáutica Civil "negotiates ASA/BASA and coordinates air connectivity" (p23); Singapore's CAAS "negotiates bilateral air service agreements" (p38). Chile's required change is to "Actively lobby for air service liberalisation and simplified visa regimes" (p28).
- System-level reading (p58): "Sustained tourism growth is not created by fragmented interventions, but by a coordinated destination system in which governance, demand generation and access reinforce one another"; key takeout "Governance is what aligns the parts".

### (d) Benchmark precedent

**In the Benchmarking Report.**
- Visa and entry friction: Kenya "accelerates demand by reducing entry friction and positioning Nairobi as a multipurpose regional and global hub" (Global Benchmarking Report, Mar 2026, p59). Chile's required change for the Western Cape is to "Actively lobby for air service liberalisation and simplified visa regimes" (p28), and the screen scored every comparator on "Ease of Access & Visa", defined as "flight entry conditions, visa openness, e-visa availability, visa-free access for Western Cape priority markets, and the clarity and efficiency of entry processes" (p11).
- Gateway infrastructure: Chile financed the ~US$900 million Nuevo Pudahuel airport expansion through a private concession won in 2015 and awarded by the Ministry of Public Works (p26). The lesson printed is "Treat air access as core economic infrastructure, not a tourism add-on" (p28).
- The deck classes visa reform with route development as demand activation, "rarely sufficient alone" (p8).

**Safety: none in the Benchmarking Report.** The nearest case is the Inclusion Plan's own Rio de Janeiro card: Rio Top Tour in Santa Marta, where access and safety were preconditions, not product; at peak 38 police units and almost 10,000 officers; security collapsed in 2017 and police shot a tourist (SME and Community Inclusion Plan, Sep 2026, p7). Its takeaway is "safety is a prerequisite" (p12).
