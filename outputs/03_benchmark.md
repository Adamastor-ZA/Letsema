# 03 Peer benchmark, gap sizing and precedents

Phase 3 deliverable. Date: 7 October 2026. All figures are live on the Benchmark, Gap sizing and Normalisation tabs of `afs_cost_benchmark.xlsx`; every row carries the data_ids of its numerator and denominator. Rand conversions use ECB period-average reference rates. Lower is better for every cost ratio. The gap figures are a hypothesis range, not a target.

## Answer first

On the comparable expense measure, AFS Insurance SA sits close to the peer median and well short of the top quartile. Total operating expenses excluding commission and claims are an estimated 19.5 percent of insurance revenue against a peer median of 22.1 percent and a top quartile of 13.3 percent across 15 IFRS 17 entities. The AFS figure rests on the assumed attributable expense share, so the comparison is only as good as that assumption. On the as-reported measure, non-attributable expenses of 11.5 percent of revenue are above the all-peer median of 6.8 percent, and the gap is entirely in the non-life book: Non-Life SA reports 12.3 percent against a non-life peer median of 3.8 percent, while Life SA at 4.0 percent is below its peer median of 13.8 percent.

Acquisition cost is not the problem once the bank fee is set aside. Including the fee, AFS acquires at 10.8 percent of revenue on the amortised view and 15.3 percent on the cash view, against a composite peer median of 10.6 percent. Excluding the R774m to Absa Bank, the cash ratio is 6.7 percent, which is at the composite top quartile of 6.5 percent. The fee is a transfer price inside the group, so this is a question of where the economics land, not of saving.

The non-life combined ratio tells the same story from the other side. Before non-attributable expenses, Non-Life SA runs at 81.5 percent against a South African non-life median of 89.5 percent, the best in the set. Including non-attributable expenses it is 93.8 percent against 93.0 percent, mid-pack. The underwriting is good and the overhead gives the advantage away.

In rand, the gap to peer median is R420m on the as-reported opex measure, R349m of it in non-life, and nil on the comparable measure, where AFS is already below the median and the stretch to top quartile is R563m. Realised at the Normalisation factors this gives a benchmark hypothesis of R210m to R556m on the opex pools, which brackets the precedent-based hypothesis of R187m to R541m from the AFS cost base tab. The two routes agree to within the width of the assumptions. Twenty precedents with hard numbers show targets of 6 to 13 percent of the addressable base over two to three years, with delivery often above target, at a cost to achieve of 0.25 to 0.7 times run-rate savings for headcount and procurement programmes and 1.5 to 2 times where platforms or licences are consolidated.

## 1. Peer set as researched

The set stands as specified. Standard Bank is shown with Liberty (the consolidated group lines) and as Liberty alone, and the consolidated row is excluded from the medians to avoid double counting. Two changes are forced by disclosure. Société Générale Assurances yielded no figures: its sites refused every retrieval and only the regulatory availability notice was obtained, so it is excluded with the document names recorded for a later attempt. Itaú's insurance segment discloses only claims and revenue lines and is excluded from the expense ratios. Discovery is represented by Discovery Life and Discovery Insure rather than the group, because the group's marketing and administration expenses are dominated by the medical scheme administration business, which has no insurance revenue to match. Hollard's latest statements are FY2023 on IFRS 4, so its IFRS 17 rows come from the KPMG survey for FY2024. Capitec Life is included but its ratios reflect a young funeral and credit life book sold through the bank, with the channel cost sitting in the bank.

Disclosure depth sets what can be compared. Every South African peer and every European peer reports IFRS 17 insurance revenue and insurance service expenses, so M1, M3 and M5 are broadly populated. The comparable expense ratio M2 needs total operating expenses by nature, which 15 entities give. Policy counts, headcount and adviser numbers are patchy; AFS discloses none, so M6 to M8 are context only. No entity discloses claims handling cost separately, and the European Solvency II reports published since 2023 show only total expenses by line of business, so the claims handling cost ratio could not be benchmarked and is a management information item. The Solvency II expense splits by type that would have given it stopped being published with the 2023 reporting changes.

## 2. Normalisation

Each row on the Benchmark tab shows the raw ratio, a factor and an adjustment (inputs, default 1.0 and 0), the normalised value, and an include flag. The rules are set out on the Normalisation tab and summarised here. Accounting basis: IFRS 17 and MFRS 17 rows enter the medians; Brazilian GAAP, IRDAI GAAP and IFRS 4 rows are shown raw and excluded, unless the metric is defined on gross written premium (M4). Business mix: rows are tagged life, non-life, composite or intermediary and the summary statistics are computed for each group, so Life SA is read against life peers, Non-Life SA against non-life peers, and Insurance SA against composites and the whole set. The savings-heavy European bancassurers are excluded from the acquisition medians, since their commissions are on unit-linked and savings volumes. Captive versus open distribution: tagged, not adjusted; the AFS acquisition ratio is shown with and without the bank fee, and Liberty's bancassurance profit share to Standard Bank is excluded as a profit transfer. Scale and financial year-end: tagged, not adjusted; the latest full year is used for each peer and interims are not annualised. Attributable allocation: the as-reported non-attributable measure (M1) is kept because it is what the market sees, but it is unreliable across entities because OUTsurance pushes about 90 percent of its operating expenses into insurance service expenses and Sanlam about a third. M2 is the comparable measure, and for AFS it depends on the attributable assumption.

Three readings follow from the precedents. First, business-wide efficiency programmes at South African and European insurers target 6 to 13 percent of the addressable cost base over two to three years, and the South African programmes (Old Mutual twice, Momentum Metropolitan twice) delivered at or above target. Second, removing a head office or holding layer after a separation releases far more, 37 percent in Prudential's case and about £95m at Old Mutual plc, which is the closest analogue to collapsing duplicated licence and control-function cost in Insurance HO. Third, cost to achieve depends on the lever mix: 0.25 to 0.7 times run-rate savings for headcount, procurement and central cost, 1.5 to 2.0 times where platform migration or licence consolidation is bundled, with capital benefits from licence mergers (Aviva £1.2bn, Phoenix £720m) reported separately from the expense case. For a centralisation that touches licences and platforms, a planning assumption of 8 to 12 percent of addressable cost over three years at 1.5 to 2 times cost to achieve is what the record supports.

Gaps in the precedent record: no primary document for the Standard Bank and Liberty integration synergies (press reports R620m a year), nothing quantified for Sanlam's Assupol licence consolidation, Santam's FutureFit restructure, or any Absa programme, and no cost base for several global programmes, so the percentage of base can be derived for only eight.
