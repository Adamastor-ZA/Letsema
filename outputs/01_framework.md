# 01 Cost opportunity framework: Absa Financial Services centralisation

Phase 1 deliverable. Status: for partner review. Date: 6 October 2026. UK English. Tags: Fact (disclosed), Derived (calculated, formula shown), Assumption (judgement, marked †).

## Answer first

The framework is a three-level tree. Level 1 cuts by cost pool, which is where each rand lands in the IFRS 17 income statement. Level 2 cuts by value chain function, which is where the rand is incurred. Level 3 cuts by lever, which is the mechanism that removes it. Each level is MECE on its own axis, and the three axes are independent, so a cost sits in exactly one cell of the Level 1 by Level 2 grid and each lever acts on it in only one way.

Of the 54 lever cells, 18 are directly enabled by centralisation, 25 are adjacent and 11 are independent. The direct cells cluster in four functions: technology, finance and actuarial and risk, governance and corporate, and investments. Claims and policy administration carry the largest cost but most of their levers are adjacent: centralisation makes them easier, not possible.

The entity map needs correcting before Phase 2. Three points in the client context are wrong or out of date. First, Rest of Africa is not yet distribution only. Absa Life Assurance Kenya and First Assurance Kenya still underwrote at 31 December 2025 and the sale was only signed in August 2026. Second, AFS no longer holds a collective investment scheme manager or a market LISP. Those went to Sanlam Investment Holdings in 2022 and to Glacier in November 2023. Third, there are four South African insurance licences, not two. A cell captive, Absa Risk Transfer Insurance Company, was licensed in January 2024, and a fourth licence, Absa Insurance Risk Management Services, was closed in 2025. The licence count matters because duplicated licences are the first structural lever.

### 1.2 Contradictions with the client context

Four points need to be put to the client.

First, Rest of Africa still underwrites. The AFS at 31 December 2025 names ALAK and FAK as underwriting entities with solo solvency covers (AFS FY25 p230, p234, p240). The Kenyan exit was agreed in August 2026 and is subject to approvals. For Phase 2 the Kenyan entities are in the FY23 to FY25 cost base and in the 1H26 interim, and must be shown separately so the continuing perimeter is clean.

Second, "investment licences" is narrower than the context implies. The asset manager, the multi-manager, the alternative manager and the unit trust manager went to Sanlam Investment Holdings in exchange for a stake that stood at 13% at FY25 (AFS FY25 p151). The market LISP went to Glacier in November 2023. What remains is AIMS as an administrative and discretionary FSP, the stockbroker, the trust company and possibly a residual Absa Fund Managers vehicle. The investments value chain in the tree is therefore a fee and platform business, not an asset manager, and the investment management cost pool is mostly fees paid out, not fees earned.

Third, there are four non-life and life licences in South Africa, with one just closed. The ARTIC cell captive licence of January 2024 is a second non-life balance sheet, with its own board and solvency cover of 2.11 (AFS FY25 p240). AIRMS was closed in 2025. The PA penalty on AIC for writing classes outside its licence (31 October 2025) shows that class-of-business boundaries between licences are enforced, which bears directly on how far underwriting can be consolidated.

Fourth, the operating model moved in 2026. The Group now reports three pan-African business units, with the former Africa Regions segment folded into PPB and BB (1H26 SENS p52). Insurance SA sits inside PPB. The integrated report records a decision to refine the model "down to three business units" (Integrated Report 2025 p10). The AFS operating model review the client describes sits inside this larger reorganisation, and the Phase 2 segment series will break at 1H26.

Two open points. The FSP numbers for Absa Trust and the stockbroker are not confirmed from a primary source, and the Absa Fund Managers status is unknown. Both are quick to close with the client or the FSCA register.

## 2. The cut chosen and why

A cost tree is MECE only if each level cuts on one axis and the axes are independent. The three axes here are nature, location and mechanism.

Level 1 cuts by nature of cost, which maps one to one onto the IFRS 17 income statement. Every expense line of an insurer lands in exactly one pool (see the mapping in section 3). This is the axis the finance function already reports on, so it reconciles to the published numbers.

Level 2 cuts by value chain function. This is the axis the operating model review used and the axis on which centralisation acts, because a shared service is a function, not a cost type. A rand of operating expense in claims handling and a rand in policy administration are the same nature but different functions, and different levers remove them.

Level 3 cuts by mechanism. The six levers come from a single decomposition. Cost equals volume multiplied by unit cost. Volume splits into work that is needed (demand) and spend that should never have been incurred (leakage). Unit cost splits into the internal cost of doing the work (productivity), the external price paid (price), the number of times a fixed cost is duplicated (consolidation) and the fixed cost that the legal and licence structure itself imposes (structural). Each lever is one and only one of these six terms, which is what makes them mutually exclusive, and together the six terms are the whole identity, which is what makes them exhaustive.

Two boundary rules keep structural and consolidation apart, because they are the pair most likely to overlap. A duplicated cost is structural if removing it requires a change to a licence, a legal entity, a board or a statutory control function. It is consolidation if it can be removed by management decision alone. Under this rule a second actuarial team is consolidation, a second head of actuarial function is structural.

## 7. What does not centralise cleanly and why

Seven constraints bind. Each is a fact about the regime, not a judgement, and each shapes the structural column.

Life and non-life cannot share a licence. The PA licenses life and non-life insurance separately, and the register of insurers shows composite licences only for microinsurers and reinsurers (Fact, PA Selected South African insurance sector data, December 2025 p4). Absa Life and AIC therefore remain two insurers with two boards, two heads of actuarial function, two external audits, two ORSAs and two solvency capital requirements. The AIC penalty of October 2025 shows that writing a class outside a licence is a contravention, so product cannot be moved between balance sheets without licence amendment.

Control functions may be shared but accountability may not. Each insurer's board remains responsible for its risk, compliance, actuarial and internal audit functions. Sharing is possible as intra-group outsourcing under the governance and outsourcing standards, with board approval and PA notification, and the regulator can require a function to be brought back in-house. Shared services therefore reduce headcount but not the number of accountable officers.

FAIS licences are entity-bound. Each FSP needs its own key individuals, compliance officer and representative register. Consolidating Instant Life, AIMS and the stockbroker into fewer FSPs means re-registering representatives and matching licence categories. It is feasible, but it is a regulatory project, not an organisational one.

Policyholder transfers need regulatory approval. Moving policies between insurers, for instance from ARTIC to AIC or between cells, requires a statutory transfer process. Absa has done this before, moving policies from Guardrisk Life to Absa Life in 2020 and 2021 (Fact, Absa transfer notices, 16 January 2020 and 27 January 2021). It takes time and actuarial and legal cost.

The cell captive is ring-fenced. ARTIC is a separate licence with its own conditions and solvency cover. Cells carry their own capital. Collapsing it into AIC is a licence and transfer decision for the PA, not an operating choice.

Binder and outsourcing rules limit how distribution fees are set. Binder fees are capped and conditioned by regulation, and material outsourcing arrangements carry notification and oversight requirements. Any repricing of the bank distribution fee or of third-party binders must sit inside those caps, which is why the price lever in distribution is adjacent rather than direct.

VAT can move the wrong way. South Africa has no group VAT registration, and financial services are largely exempt, so VAT charged on intra-group recharges is mostly irrecoverable. Centralising services into one entity and recharging the others can create VAT cost that did not exist when each entity employed its own staff. This needs tax input before any shared service case is signed off, and it is why the leakage cell in governance is marked as possibly negative.

Two further perimeter constraints. In-country insurance and bancassurance regulation in Kenya and the other markets keeps distribution staff and licences local, so only design, analytics and vendor terms can be centralised for Rest of Africa. And data sharing between the bank and the insurers is subject to POPIA, which constrains how far a single customer and lead platform can go.

## 8. One worked example per lever, with the metric that would evidence it

Structural. The closure of the AIRMS licence in 2025 removed one board, one head of actuarial function, one audit and one set of returns. The same logic applies to ARTIC. The metric is cost per licence (board fees, control function cost, audit fee, levies) and, for trapped capital, own funds above target cover in each entity multiplied by the cost of equity. The FY25 solo covers of 1.51, 1.81 and 2.11 against a group cover of 1.49 (Fact, AFS FY25 p240) show capital is distributed unevenly across the three South African insurers. Whether that is trapped depends on each board's target, which is management information.

Consolidation. One policy administration platform for Absa Life, Instant Life and AIC in place of separate stacks. The metric is administration cost per in-force policy, and the number of core platforms and vendors before and after.

Productivity. Straight-through processing of new business and low-value claims. The metrics are the straight-through rate, cost per transaction and, for distribution, annual premium equivalent per adviser. Spans and layers are evidenced by the ratio of managers to staff and the number of layers from chief executive to front line.

Demand. Failure demand in the contact centre. The metric is the share of contacts that are repeat, avoidable or caused by an upstream error, and the number of product versions and rate tables in force.

Price. Reinsurance placed once for the insurance group rather than entity by entity. The metric is net reinsurance cost as a share of insurance revenue, and for suppliers the unit price per repair, assessment or licence before and after.

Leakage. A claims leakage audit on a sample of paid claims. The metric is leakage as a share of claims paid, split into overpayment, fraud and missed recovery. For premium collection the metric is the share of debit orders that fail and are not recovered within the month.

## 9. MECE test and its result

The test was run twice, once on each axis and once across the grid.

On the Level 1 axis, each IFRS 17 expense line was assigned to one pool (section 3). Two lines could have landed in two pools. Claims handling cost is attributable expense under IFRS 17 and sits with claims, but it is operating expense by nature, so it goes to pool 3. Reinsurance was named in the client's pool 5 and also under the price lever, so it is measured once in pool 1 and worked by the structural and price levers. Both resolutions are recorded in the table.

On the Level 2 axis, the nine functions were checked against the segment description of Insurance SA (AFS FY25 p161) and the segment changes note (AFS FY25 p63) and against the IFRS 17 attributable expense categories. Reinsurance structuring sits in product and pricing, not claims. Collections sit in policy administration, not distribution. Both are stated in section 4.

On the Level 3 axis, the six levers were derived from the volume and unit cost identity in section 2, with the structural and consolidation boundary rule applied. One residual overlap remained: a fee renegotiation that is only possible because volumes have been combined is both price and consolidation. The rule adopted is that the saving is counted as price and the enabler is recorded as consolidation, so it appears once.

Across the grid, three gaps were found and closed. Credit impairment on premium debtors had no home and now sits in pool 3 under leakage. Depreciation and amortisation had no home and now sit in pool 3 under technology and premises. Cost of capital is not an accounting cost and would have inflated the addressable base, so it is reported in pool 5 and kept out of the accounting total.

Two perimeter gaps remain open for the partner. The bank-side cost of bancassurance distribution (branch and digital staff who sell insurance) sits in Absa Bank, not in AFS. Fees the underwriters pay the bank are intra-group and net to nil at group level. The POV must decide whether the cost base is AFS as a set of legal entities or bancassurance as an economic activity. The tree works for either, but the addressable base differs materially between them.
